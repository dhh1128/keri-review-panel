# 02 — Security Model and Threat Posture

## Thesis

KERI's security model is not the model most reviewers arrive with. It does not promise that attacks cannot happen; it promises that when they happen the identifier survives, the misbehavior becomes evidence, and control can be recovered. Its objective function is *mission survivability*, not invulnerability; its primary mechanism is *detection*, not prevention; its characteristic property is that duplicity is *evident* (provable and attributable), not that duplicity is *resistant* (made impossible); and it assumes from the outset that the controller itself may be malicious. These are not hedges or admissions of weakness. They are deliberate design choices with named, load-bearing consequences: because KERI declines to buy global consensus, it accepts that different validators may hold different-but-valid histories of the same identifier, possibly forever; because it treats forks as anticipated disturbances rather than protocol failures, it moves the detection responsibility into an observer layer (watchers, jurors, judges) that the protocol enables but does not guarantee. The single most common review error is to evaluate KERI against an invulnerability objective function it never adopted -- to criticize it for not preventing what it was built to make detectable. As the corpus puts it, that "is analogous to criticizing a smoke detector for not preventing fires." (`dg-c02-claude.md` §5) This section states the posture precisely, separates what is established from what is genuinely contested, flags the assumptions each guarantee is conditional on, and binds the six Del Giudice claims (DG-C01..C06) to what KERI actually does and does not provide.

## 1. Survivability, not invulnerability

The most load-bearing frame in the entire corpus is the choice of objective function. The security-analysis method requires every analyst to declare, before assessing any claim, which objective the claim assumes, because the two objectives evaluate the same facts to opposite verdicts:

- An **invulnerability-oriented** framing "assumes that security requires prevention of entire classes of attack (e.g., forks, equivocation, conflicting histories) via mechanisms such as global ordering or consensus." (`standard-analysis.md` §0)
- A **survivability-oriented** framing "does value prevention" but "assumes that some adversarial behaviors are inevitable and evaluates security in terms of a gestalt... invulnerability, detectability, evidence preservation, recoverability, and mission-level continuity under disturbance." (`standard-analysis.md` §0)

KERI, as specified, "adopts a survivability-oriented security model." (`standard-analysis.md` §0) The doctrinal source is explicit: "KERI is explicitly **not** designed around invulnerability. Its security posture is more accurately understood through the lens of **mission survivability**" -- "a framework common in military and safety-critical systems engineering." (`background.md` §4.5.1) The properties a survivable-secure system aims for are: continue functioning during and after adversarial disturbance; make hostile actions observable and attributable; preserve evidence enabling recovery, containment, and governance; and "Avoid abortive mission failure even when attacks succeed." (`background.md` §4.5.1)

The reframing rule that follows is the crux: "Forks and equivocation by malicious controllers are *anticipated disturbances*, not protocol failures." (`background.md` §4.5.1) This is why the corpus issues a standing category-error warning: "Failure to align the evaluator's objective function with KERI's stated survivability-oriented goals risks category errors that systematically mischaracterize KERI's security properties." (`background.md` §4.5.1)

The papers make the same commitment in engineering terms. "no system can guarantee resistance to all intrusions... detection and recovery are therefore first-class design requirements, not fallbacks." (`wtbo.md` §3, citing CMU SEI survivable-systems work) The deployment doctrine states it as a business fact: "If our business grows, we will eventually be hacked." (`origin-security.md` §1) The correct posture is to plan for breach rather than pretend it will not occur. And the resilience is instantiated concretely -- pre-rotation makes control "fundamentally resilient, not just resistant" (`kspqs.md` §Recovery and Invalidation, L88 @`181569b64`); the key phrase across the papers is **resilient, not resistant**. Read alone, that sentence states an unconditional guarantee, and the same author states the same guarantee conditionally elsewhere. So wherever the *strength* of pre-rotation is at issue rather than its rhetorical shape, this chapter cites `wtbo.md` §4 and not `kspqs.md` -- see §5.

**Established.** That KERI is survivability-oriented rather than invulnerability-oriented is not contested within the corpus; it is stated doctrine in both the security analysis and the primary papers, and it is the frame against which every downstream claim must be read.

## 2. Detection, not prevention

Survivability at the mechanism level reduces to a preference for detection over prevention. The definitions are crisp: "**Prevention**: making an attack impossible / **Detection**: making an attack observable." (`background.md` §4.5) "KERI is fundamentally a **detection-oriented system** with optional response mechanisms." (`background.md` §4.5) The corollary is an analytical hygiene rule with teeth: "Any claim that implies *prevention* must be scrutinized for hidden assumptions." (`background.md` §4.5)

The rationale is a deliberate cost decision, not a capability gap. KERI declines to pay for prevention because prevention has a price it refuses. "guaranteeing prevention requires global consensus at a cost in latency and scalability that both systems deliberately decline to pay." (`cr-ad-trust.md` §3) In CAP-theorem terms, "KERI explicitly chooses availability and partition tolerance, and handles the consistency gaps through detection." (`wtbo.md` §5) Detection buys low latency while still yielding provable dishonesty: "Detection (rather than prevention) allows KERI to operate with low latency while ensuring that any dishonesty is provable." (`keri-primer.md` §2.6)

This is precisely the point where Certificate Transparency and KERI rhyme, and where reviewers who know CT should recognize the shape. Both CT and KERI "are fundamentally detection-oriented. Neither prevents a bad actor." (`cr-ad-trust.md` §3) The KERI spec itself sharpens the distinction against CT: "Unlike certificate transparency, KERI enables the detection of Duplicity in the Key state via nonrepudiable cryptographic proofs of Duplicity, not merely the detection of inconsistency... that MAY or MAY NOT be duplicitous." (KERI spec, spec-body.md §Overcoming existing security overlay flaws, L85) CT detects that two logs disagree; KERI detects that one controller signed two conflicting things, which is a nonrepudiable proof of fault rather than a mere inconsistency.

**Established, with a standing invariant.** The keripy knowledge base encodes the slogan as a protocol-level (version-independent) claim: KERI is a "decentralized key-state *continuity* mechanism, not a consensus protocol, ledger, or global-ordering system." It "does **not** make a malicious controller's fork *impossible*; it makes it **detectable and attributable by observers**." (`00-lens.md` claim 2) The code consequence is that no verification path may "acquire a *trust* dependency on an external service" -- witnesses and OOBIs are "sources of **data, never sources of authority**." (`00-lens.md` claim 1)

## 3. Duplicity-evident, not duplicity-resistant

The compression of "detection not prevention" into a single term is the pair *duplicity-evident vs duplicity-resistant*. KERI's posture "is best described as *duplicity-evident* rather than *duplicity-resistant*. It doesn't try to make duplicity impossible... It makes duplicity *provable and attributable*." (`cr-ad-trust.md` §4) The same framing appears verbatim in the papers: KERI "doesn't try to make duplicity impossible -- that would require global consensus... It makes duplicity *provable and attributable*." (`wtbo.md` §4)

**Definition.** Duplicity is "the existence of more than one Version of a Verifiable KEL for a given AID." (KERI spec, termdefs) Operationally, it is a controller "signing two different events with the same sequence number" (`keri-primer.md` §2.6) -- the identity-layer analogue of the double-spend problem. Critically, duplicity requires *two fully-signed-and-witnessed* conflicting versions, so it "is only possible via key compromise or the controller acting duplicitously (indistinguishable to a watcher)." (KERI spec, L51) Duplicity (conflicting-but-valid) must be distinguished from invalidity (`background.md` §4.1.2): an unsigned or malformed event is simply dropped; a duplicitous event is *valid* and therefore evidence.

**The terminological trap.** The most cited critical framing of this posture is Del Giudice's: "KERI is therefore not duplicity-resistant but only duplicity-evident." (2025 ETH Zurich thesis Ch.4 §4.1 p.38, quoted in `background.md` §8.2 DG-C03) Whether that sentence is a criticism depends entirely on the reading of "resistant." If "resistant" means prevention, the statement is accurate and faithful. If "resistant" is read to include detection-plus-response (i.e., survivability), the phrasing *understates* KERI's model. The word "only" subtly imports an invulnerability bias. (`dg-c03-claude.md` §2, §5) A blockchain-background reader is especially prone to hear "not duplicity-resistant" as "weaker than a blockchain," which is a category error: KERI "solves a different problem." (`dg-c03-claude.md` §6)

**Why evidence is not a consolation prize.** The corpus is emphatic that detectability is not an inferior substitute for prevention: the rhetorical move "'at best, duplicity may become detectable' treats detectability as inferior to prevention without arguing *why* prevention is required given KERI's threat model." (`dg-c02-claude.md` §5) In deployment, the evidence is fatal: a watcher that finds conflicting log-heads "can broadcast both events as cryptographic proof of fraud... this proof is fatal to the reputation of the identifier." (`keri-primer.md` §2.6) The spec grounds this in the KEL's structure: a malicious controller "producing a detectably duplicitous event history is tantamount to a detectable total exploit... analogous to a total but detectable exploit of any BFT ledger such as a detectable 51% attack on a proof-of-work ledger." (KERI spec, L1842) One valid copy is enough: "One verifiable copy of a KEL (more specificly a KERL) is enough to detect duplicity in any other verifiable but inconsistent copy." (KERI spec, L1719)

## 4. The zero-trust, malicious-controller stance

KERI's threat model does not extend the controller any benefit of the doubt. "Verifiers do **not** assume controller honesty." Controllers "May be honest or malicious (analysis distinguishes both cases)." (`background.md` §2.1) Protecting against a fully malicious controller is an explicit non-goal: "**Protection against a fully malicious controller.**" (`background.md` §1.3, Explicit Non-Goals) The spec agrees that the controller "may also be malicious and, in some ways, may be indistinguishable from a successful malicious third party." (KERI spec, L1723)

This is zero-trust in the strict sense: "end-verifiability is a prerequisite for true zero-trust computing infrastructure, where zero-trust means never trust always verify." (KERI spec, L103) The keripy knowledge base turns the stance into a coding rule: "every externally supplied event, signature, key, receipt, or credential is **hostile input until verified**... 'looks well-formed' is not 'is authorized.'" (`00-lens.md` claim 3)

The witness set does not rescue a malicious controller's victims, and reviewers must not assume it does. When the controller is malicious it "may produce multiple conflicting KELs," and "each may independently achieve sufficient agreement"; "**Witness thresholding alone does not prevent this.**" Security therefore "shifts from **consistency** to **detectability**." (`background.md` §5.8) The reason is structural: a malicious controller "can simply run its own witnesses" (thesis Ch.3 §3.1.4 p.26, in `background.md` §8.2), so a witness threshold does not bind an adversary who controls the witnesses. The trust model is adversarial toward witnesses too: "witnesses are assumed to be potentially malicious." (`keri-primer.md` §2.6) A witness "makes no such assertion. A witness simply stores and serves events" (`keri-primer.md` §2.6) -- "a fundamentally different role than a CA." (`cr-ad-trust.md` §4)

**The honest-controller subtlety.** The single most-misread point in the formal literature is the honest-controller assumption. It is a proof-technique simplification, not a KERI deployment requirement. "Removing it does not break KERI's security -- it shifts the focus from prevention to detection and recovery." (`dg-c06-claude.md` §3) Malicious behavior "does not 'break' KERI; it triggers detection mechanisms." (`dg-c06-claude.md` §5) A formal analysis that assumes an honest controller to prove strong consistency is describing the *proof's* scope, not asserting that KERI requires honesty in the field. Conflating the two makes KERI "appear **less secure than it is**." (`dg-c02-claude.md` §5)

**Established.** The zero-trust stance and the malicious-controller non-goal are stated identically across the security analysis, the primary papers, the spec, and the reference-implementation knowledge base. What is *contested by outsiders* -- and resolved by objective-function alignment -- is whether the non-goal is a flaw. It is not; it is a deliberate boundary.

**What a fail-closed decision looks like in the code.** `[K]` The hostile-input rule has a machine-checkable form: a verifier that meets input it does not know how to evaluate must refuse rather than fall back to a default. keripy at `b8f60166b` does exactly that for the two ACDC edge operators it recognizes but has not implemented, and states the reason in the source: DI2I and NOT "are listed so they fail closed diagnosably instead of being dropped and silently defaulting" (`src/keri/vdr/verifying.py` L38-39 @`b8f60166b`, read for this chapter). Both raise `ValidationError` rather than escrowing, because escrowing "would promise a retry that can never succeed" (L445). That is the kind of design decision this chapter asks a reviewer to look for -- the implementation saying, about itself, which way it falls when it does not know.

**And the seam, which cuts the other way.** `[K]` The same commit also contains the opposite disposition for the same input, and the pair is the finding. `vdr/verifying.py` filters an unrecognized operator token out of the list and then applies the default -- `ops = [cand for cand in ops if cand in self.UnaryOps]` (L432), under a comment reading "Tokens this verifier does not recognize are skipped" (L430). `acdc/ipexing.py` treats the identical input as malformed and refuses: "A provided but unrecognized operator fails closed instead of being treated like an omitted one" (L761-762), implemented as `elif op not in UnaryEdgeOps: return None` (L765-766). One path therefore fails open on an unrecognized edge operator and the other fails closed; the same edge can pass credential processing and fail IPEX grant verification, or the reverse; and neither file acknowledges the other. Which disposition is intended is unsettled and nothing in the tree decides it. A fail-open and a fail-closed path for one input is a threat-posture finding whichever way it resolves, and a reviewer meeting an argument that leans on either module's strictness should first ask which module the argument is about.

## 5. The pre-rotation firewall: survivability instantiated

Pre-rotation is where survivability stops being a philosophy and becomes a mechanism. It is "KERI's most important security innovation." (`cr-ad-trust.md` §4) On every establishment event the controller publishes the current signing key's public value but, for the next key, "only a cryptographic hash of its public key. The next key itself stays offline, in cold storage." (`cr-ad-trust.md` §4) Control authority is thereby split: "control authority is split between keypairs that hold signing authority and keypairs that hold rotation authority." (KERI spec, §Pre-rotation, L1364)

The firewall property is the payoff: "An attacker who steals the current key can sign events, but cannot rotate to take permanent control: they don't know the next key's value, only its hash." (`cr-ad-trust.md` §4) The forward-blind commitment means "later exploit of the current authoritative signing key(s) may not capture key rotation authority as it has already been transferred via the pre-commitment to a new unexposed set of keys." (KERI spec, L1628) The residual attack surface collapses to "a side-channel attack on the private key store itself and not on signing infrastructure." (KERI spec, L1391)

The asymmetry between the two compromise cases is a *design property*, encoded as an invariant, not a runtime check: "Compromise of *current* keys is survivable; compromise of *pre-rotation* keys is catastrophic and unrecoverable." (`00-lens.md` claim 4; `30-invariants.md` C2, citing `background.md` §3.4-3.5) If both the current signing keys and the pre-committed rotation keys are captured, "no cryptographic mechanism can restore control. The identifier must be abandoned." (`wtbo.md` §5) This pain is real but bounded, and the corpus is careful to note that abandon-and-reissue "is also the *only* recovery mode X.509 supports for *any* key compromise." (`wtbo.md` §5)

Two honesty caveats attach. First, the guarantee is a race, not a wall: "the hash protects the *next* key, never the one currently exposed, so safety rests on the controller detecting compromise and rotating before an adversary can exploit what the rotation reveals." (`wtbo.md` §4, L99 @`181569b64`) That sentence is the citation of record for the strength of the pre-rotation guarantee anywhere in this corpus, in preference to `kspqs.md`, which describes the same mechanism as leaving the attacker "always one step behind" and reads alone as unconditional. Specification text has independently arrived at the same concession -- on the forward branch, not the standardizing one, so it binds nobody yet: hiding pre-rotated keys behind digests "protects the next key pair but does not protect the currently exposed one", and the two defences are "complementary, not alternatives; pre-rotation continues to provide defense in depth for whichever signature scheme is in use" (CESR specification `v1.1` §Post-Quantum Cryptographic Primitives, L1553 @`65fd7518b`) `[N1.1]`. Two sources arriving at one hedge from different directions is why this chapter states the guarantee conditionally rather than as a wall. Second, recovery does not launder accountability. Because events are nonrepudiably signed, "any key compromise is still the responsibility of the controller"; recovery repairs the KEL only for *future* validators, while those who already first-saw the compromised events still saw them. (KERI spec, L1792)

**The post-quantum hedge, which this chapter has not carried until now.** The firewall's post-quantum property is a claim about hashes, it is `[N]`, and nothing in this pass weakens it: because the prior-next key digests are post-quantum safe "in that a quantum computer is not advantaged over a conventional computer in inverting cryptographic strength digests", a surprise quantum attacker who inverts the exposed signing keys "is not able to forge a verifiable Rotation event" (KERI specification §SQAR, L1580 @`fbdd4a615`). Grover buys a quadratic speedup against a hash where Shor buys an exponential one against RSA and ECC (`cr-ad-trust.md` §3), and that asymmetry is the whole architectural bet. What has moved is not the bet but the *number* the papers attach to it. The figure the corpus repeats is that a 256-bit hash "provides an effective 128 bits of post-quantum security" (`kspqs.md` §The Pre-Rotation Mechanism, L72 @`181569b64`), and in the same paragraph the author now records that ENISA's draft guidance "does ask for 384-bit hash outputs where quantum security is sought, on the ground that a 256-bit output's quantum security level could arguably drop below 128 bits" (same line). His answer is that this is "a parameter choice, not a change of primitive, and one KERI's agility accommodates by design" (same line) -- an agility argument rather than a defence of the number, and his assessment rather than a surveyed result. The practical consequence for a reviewer: someone who has read ENISA and puts the 128-bit figure back to you is not making a category error, and the honest reply concedes the parameter and argues the architecture. Do not defend the number.

Two counterweights keep that fair, and neither of them is this corpus's own advocacy. The same European guidance that contests the number independently supports the primitive: "the new lattice-based schemes must be combined with a classically secure mechanism before they carry security on their own, while hash-based mechanisms may be used standalone" (`kspqs.md` §The Pre-Rotation Mechanism, L72 @`181569b64`, reporting ENISA's *Agreed Cryptographic Mechanisms* v2.0) -- a regulator treating hashes as standalone-safe and lattice post-quantum cryptography as not yet, which is precisely the bet pre-rotation makes. And the forward specification branch has stopped implying that pre-rotation alone is a complete post-quantum answer, conceding the exposed-key limit in its own words and stacking post-quantum signatures beside pre-rotation rather than in place of it (CESR `v1.1` §Post-Quantum Cryptographic Primitives, L1553 @`65fd7518b`) `[N1.1]`; that text is not on `main` and must not be cited as normative. Two things the corpus does not know and must not guess: whether KERI/CESR's digest code points can express a 384-bit commitment in the pre-rotation `n` field without a specification change, and whether the deployed ecosystem uses anything but Blake3-256.

The load-bearing implementation risk sits here too, and it survives this pass with its framing corrected. The pre-rotation commitment check "**Never weaken or short-circuit**" (`30-invariants.md` C1), yet keripy's `eventing.py:exposeds` can go *permissive* on a digest-code mismatch: a wrong committed digest code causes the revealed key to be "silently excluded from threshold satisfaction rather than failing closed -- weakening the pre-rotation check (the firewall, C1)" (`31-landmines.md` L6, flagged HIGH, at the knowledge base's pin `9489ac9bd`, whose own code baseline is keripy `60ab9e08` -- 493 commits behind the pin that follows). The mechanism was read for this chapter at keripy `b8f60166b` and is unchanged there: `exposeds` re-digests each revealed key using the *committed* digest's code -- `kdig = Diger(ser=siger.verfer.qb64b, code=diger.code).qb64` -- and appends the `ondex` only `if kdig == diger.qb64` (`src/keri/core/eventing.py` L2988-2990 @`b8f60166b`) `[K]`. There is no else branch, so a mismatch drops that index out of threshold satisfaction silently, and the `except IndexError: continue` just above it (L2983-2984) sits directly on top of a commented-out `ValidationError` that was deliberately not raised. A reviewer of any change touching this path must treat a fail-open here as a security defect even if the tests pass.

What does **not** survive is the urgency that framing used to carry, and the correction matters because the urgency was doing rhetorical work. The landmine is written as advice about work in flight -- new digest codes widening the surface as a Falcon pull request lands -- and no such work exists. keripy at `b8f60166b` contains no post-quantum codes at all: a case-insensitive repository-wide search of `src/keri` for FN-DSA, ML-DSA, SLH-DSA, Dilithium, SPHINCS, Kyber and ML-KEM returns **zero** matches, `MatterCodex` carries no post-quantum entries, and the only occurrences of the word *falcon* in `src/keri` are seven files importing the HTTP framework of that name, none of them under `src/keri/core`. The knowledge base is candid about why the work has not started: its own plan defers the flagship and names its prerequisite as "resolve the two conflicting Falcon spec docs first" (`PLAN.md` §7 @`9489ac9bd`). The two documents disagree on the shape of the thing -- `falcon-impl-plan.md` wants "fixed-size sigs" and "no digest-AIDs", `prompt-falcon-pr.md` wants "variable-size sigs + digest-AID" (`PLAN.md` §9 @`9489ac9bd`) -- so the design question is open, and a chapter that says anything about KERI's post-quantum plans should say that rather than pick a side. So "new digest codes widen this surface" stands as a **forward-looking hazard** and the discipline it names is correct and unaffected; an imminent-PR reading of it is not. Keep the discipline, drop the urgency, and do not let a reviewer be told that a post-quantum change is in flight in keripy.

## 6. Guarantees are stated relative to assumptions (A1-A13)

The corpus insists that no KERI security property may be stated without its assumptions and scope: "Security properties must be stated **relative to assumptions**." "Agreement thresholds do not imply global safety." "Failure to state these boundaries results in overstated guarantees." (`background.md` §4.7, §5.10) The assumption catalog is the set of axes along which every guarantee is conditional. It is load-bearing; a reviewer should treat each named assumption as a place where a real deployment can silently fall short.

| **A2** | Bounded Witness Faults | "At most `f` witnesses behave arbitrarily for a given identifier." | §4.6 |
| **A3** | Honest Controller (Conditional) | "The controller follows the protocol except where explicitly stated." Many analyses, including Del Giudice's KAWA analysis, implicitly rely on A3. | §4.6 |

Three assumptions deserve special emphasis because they are the ones reviewers and deployers most often assume for free:

- **A3 (Honest Controller) is a proof convenience, not a deployment premise.** The strongest KAWA results depend on it; the field does not. Reading A3 as "KERI requires honest controllers" is the DG-C06 misread corrected in §8 below.
- **A5 (Observer Presence) MUST NOT be silently assumed.** The qualification is verbatim doctrine: "A5 is optional in practice and MUST NOT be silently assumed." (`background.md` §4.6) Any argument that duplicity *will* be detected is smuggling in A5.
- **A8-A11 (the observer coverage/diligence/flow/integrity quartet) are not enforced by KERI at all.** "None of A8-A11 are enforced by KERI." (`background.md` §6.4) They are optional deployment properties, never protocol guarantees.

## 7. KAWA: what witness agreement is (P1-P4) and is not (P5-P8)

KAWA is KERI's Algorithm for Witness Agreement -- "a single-phase agreement protocol that provides fault tolerance without the multi-phase commit overhead of classical Byzantine Fault Tolerant algorithms like PBFT." (`cr-ad-trust.md` §4) It gives availability and fault tolerance without a blockchain. Its thresholds are `N` (total witnesses) and `M` (the receipt threshold, the *toad*). The common BFT sizing N = 3f+1, M = 2f+1 is common but not mandated: KERI is deliberately less prescriptive than BFT, and a controller may set M = N, a simple majority, or even M = 1. (`dg-c01-claude.md` §2) Treating BFT thresholds as mandatory overstates KERI's constraint.

**Provided properties (all scoped to an honest controller unless noted).** (`background.md` §5.6)

- **P1 -- Validity (Event Authenticity):** "If a correct witness accepts an establishment event, that event was authorized by the controller." Depends A1, A3, A4.
- **P2 -- Consistency (No Forks Under Honest Control):** "Two correct witnesses will not accept different establishment events at the same sequence number." Depends A2, A3, A4, A7. "This is the strongest KAWA property and is *conditional*. It does not hold if the controller is malicious."
- **P3 -- Durability (Immutability After Agreement):** "Once an event has a sufficient agreement, it cannot be removed or replaced." Depends A2, A4, A6. "Durability is local to witnesses and does not imply global convergence."
- **P4 -- Availability:** "Validators can retrieve the KERL even if the controller is offline." Depends A2, A6. "Availability degrades with witness churn or witness set mismanagement."

**Explicitly NOT provided by KAWA.** (`background.md` §5.7)

- **P5 -- Fork Prevention for Malicious Controllers**
- **P6 -- Global Consistency Across Validators**
- **P7 -- Timely Fork Detection**
- **P8 -- Automatic Fork Resolution**

"Any claim implying these properties must introduce **additional mechanisms** (e.g., observers, governance, social response)." (`background.md` §5.7) The division of labor is exact: "KAWA alone determines *local durability*. Observers determine *global detectability*." (`background.md` §5.9) Without observers, KAWA degrades to "Each validator sees *a* consistent history." (`background.md` §5.9)

**Microledgers, not blockchain -- the KAWA framing reviewers most often botch.** This is the shibboleth, and it must be stated explicitly. Blockchains carry a global ledger and global consensus that decides *whether and in what global order an event gets recorded* -- inclusion plus total ordering across all participants. KERI splits that single "blockchain in the sky" into per-identifier microledgers (the KELs), each rooted in its own identifier and held by witnesses and wallets. In KERI the event is **already recorded** by the controller on its own microledger before KAWA runs. KAWA does not ask "should this be recorded?" It asks a different, post-hoc question: is any decentralized actor trying to fork reality *after the fact*? That is detection of equivocation, not prevention of it -- of a piece with survivability-not-invulnerability. (DECENTRALIZATION-PURITY-STEERING §The thesis, human steering note; grounded in KERI spec §KAWA insight)

The spec supplies the mechanical justification for why local ordering suffices where a blockchain would demand global ordering. "because the controller is the sole source of truth for the creation of any and all key events, it alone, is sufficient to order its own key events." Key events are "idempotent authorization operations as opposed to non-idempotent account balance decrement or increment operations. Total or global ordering may be critical for non-idempotency, whereas local ordering may be sufficient for idempotency." (KERI spec, §KAWA insight, L1836) Hence "security guarantees from KAWA may approach that of other BFT algorithms but without their scalability, cost, throughput, or latency limitations." (KERI spec, L1836) A reviewer who asks "where is the chain / why no consensus layer?" has reached for blockchain intuitions in a system that deliberately does not have them, and will be dismissed with justification. (DECENTRALIZATION-PURITY-STEERING §The shibboleth)

**The immunity constraint (the one thing witness thresholds *do* buy).** For N witnesses there is a threshold M < N guaranteeing at most one sufficient agreement (or none), despite a dishonest controller, provided at most F* = N - M are unavailable and F < M are duplicitous -- so "the service may not produce multiple divergent but proper KERL." (KERI spec, L1866-1868) The validator, not the controller, selects M to make the service immune, thereby protecting itself. But this holds only within the stated fault bounds; colluding controller-plus-witnesses violates the <=F assumption, and "KAWA can't protect -- protection then comes from validator-side duplicity detection across a large diverse observer set." (KERI spec, L1874)

## 8. Witnesses, backers, and thresholds

A **witness** is "a server the controller designates to store and serve the KEL." On validating an event it returns a signed receipt; once a threshold (toad) of receipts is collected, "the event is stable." Crucially, "Witnesses make no assertion about identity... That is a fundamentally different role than a CA." (`cr-ad-trust.md` §4) A witness is under the ultimate control of the AID's controller, who may change the witness pool at will. (KERI spec, termdefs) A **backer** is an alternative to a witness, commonly using a distributed ledger to store the KEL. (KERI spec, termdefs)

The witness/toad rules are invariants:

- **TOAD bounds.** A non-empty witness list requires 1 <= toad <= len(wits); an empty list requires toad == 0. (`30-invariants.md` F1; `background.md` §4.2, §5.3)
- **Sufficient agreement = receipts meeting TOAD, not all witnesses.** This is the spec rule (the "threshold of accountable duplicity"): "the controller accepts accountability for an event when any subset M of the N witnesses confirms that event." (KERI spec, L1846, L1850) The reference implementation violates this: keripy's `WitnessReceiptor` waits for ALL witnesses rather than TOAD, "that is a defect, not the invariant," and is the likely root of at least two open issues (`30-invariants.md` F2; `31-landmines.md` L9). That claim was made against keripy `60ab9e08` and is **re-anchored here at `b8f60166b`**, where it still holds: `completed = len(wigers) == len(wits)` with the loop gated on `if len(wigers) != len(wits)`, and no reference to `hab.kever.toader` anywhere in the module (`src/keri/app/agenting.py` L360-361 @`b8f60166b`, read for this chapter) `[K]`.
- **Threshold discipline M > f (A7).** M = 1 provides no fault tolerance and "can be trivially broken by a single malicious witness." (`background.md` §5.3; `dg-c01-claude.md` §3)
- **Witness backers MUST be non-transferable.** Backer AIDs, when serving as witnesses, "MUST be non-transferable, fully qualified public keys" so a validator can verify a witness signature straight from the AID with no witness KEL required. (KERI spec, L353, L1252) Treating this as a discretionary simplification is "a characterization error *about KERI-as-specified*." (`background.md` §2.3) A subtle implementation landmine intersects here: keripy hardcodes `Ed25519N` as the prefix code for any non-transferable AID, which is correct for Ed25519 but wrong for a digest-based scheme; non-transferability in KERI "is expressed by an empty next-key commitment (`ncount=0`, `nsith='0'`), not by a special prefix code." (`31-landmines.md` L1) **Re-anchored at `b8f60166b`**, where the hardcode is still present at both sites -- `if not transferable:` sets `ncount = 0`, `nsith = '0'` and `code = MtrDex.Ed25519N` in `Hab.incept` (L2937-2940), and `nsith = '0'` with the same `code = MtrDex.Ed25519N` in `GroupHab.incept` (L3748-3750) (`src/keri/app/habbing.py` @`b8f60166b`, read for this chapter) `[K]`. This is a hazard for any future digest-based signature scheme, not a defect in flight; see §5 on why no such scheme exists in keripy today.

A standing hygiene rule against a common overreach: "Agreement thresholds do not imply global safety." (`background.md` §4.7) A toad met is local durability achieved, nothing more.

## 9. The observer layer: watchers, jurors, judges, and ambient duplicity detection

Because KAWA provides only local durability, global *detectability* is the job of an observer layer that the protocol enables but does not mandate. The three roles are deliberately separated so that no single observer both detects duplicity and rules on it. (`background.md` §6.2)

- **Watcher.** Monitors identifiers by "Collecting KERLs from multiple sources / Comparing observed histories / Detecting conflicts at identical sequence numbers." It is validator-selected, with "No required coverage or completeness / No protocol-mandated response." (`background.md` §6.2) In the spec's terms, a watcher keeps a copy of a KERL but is *not* designated by the controller; it is "Under control of the VALIDATOR, not the controller," and follows first-seen. (KERI spec, termdefs)
- **Juror (Jury).** "records evidence of duplicity, including: Conflicting events / Witness receipts / Proofs of equivocation." It has "No authority to resolve conflicts" but "Enables post hoc accountability" -- it keeps a Duplicitous Event Log (DEL) and may be a fault-tolerant pool. (`background.md` §6.2; KERI spec, L51, L1858)
- **Judge.** "applies policy or governance rules to: Rank conflicting KERLs / Recommend acceptance or rejection / Trigger recovery or remediation." It is "Policy-driven / Non-universal / May disagree across validators." (`background.md` §6.2)

**"Super" names scope, not a feature set, and it attaches to all three roles.** Where this corpus has said "super watcher" it has been inheriting `wtbo.md`'s usage, in which the word marks the *archival-service* deployment variant of a watcher (`wtbo.md` §5, and §11 below). KERI's maintainers use it differently, and the difference is worth carrying because the loose reading invites exactly the role collapse this section exists to prevent. Answering a draft KID that had defined super watchers by an enumerated feature list — comprehensive polling, push notification, merkle-checkpointed diffs, reputation endpoints — Samuel Smith rejected the framing outright: "the term super watcher seems to be any watcher that has features not in the most basic form of watcher, while many of the features listed above are actually associated with say a Juror and we should then define Jurors and some with a Judge. In my mind what makes a watcher, juror, or judge 'super' is the scope of the KELs it is watching therefore we could have super watchers or super jurors or super judges." Philip Feairheller concurred and supplied the concrete case: "a vLEI Super Watcher would have the scope of all AIDs in that ecosystem." (WebOfTrust/kids#1, 2023-11-05 and 2023-11-06.) **Provenance caveat, since this tier is not in the key above:** these are maintainer statements in a GitHub discussion, on a draft that was never merged, and they sit well below `[N]` — they are recorded here as how the term's own authors use it, not as specification text. Two consequences follow. **Super juror** and **super judge** are coherent terms this corpus has otherwise had no room for, so a deployment presented only as a "super watcher" may be doing juror or judge work under a name that conceals the role separation above; ask which of the three it performs. And *scope* is the criterion that actually does the work in the centralization argument of §11 — what makes the GLEIF super watcher a trust concentration is that one operator's view spans an entire ecosystem, not that it keeps an archive.

The detection mechanic is simple and trust-free: if a watcher "asks two witnesses for the head of the same identifier and gets different hash values, it has detected *duplicity*... The watcher can broadcast both events as cryptographic proof of fraud. That proof doesn't require trusting any authority's interpretation -- only the mathematics of the signatures." (`cr-ad-trust.md` §4)

**Ambient duplicity detection** is the emergent, non-guaranteed property that equivocation "becomes observable *somewhere* in the network, given sufficient observer activity and information flow. This is **not** guaranteed by protocol mechanics." It depends on observer presence, diligence, information overlap, and time. (`background.md` §6.3) The spec ties this to the asymmetry it creates for the defender: "a successful dead attack requires the isolation of a validator from ambient sources of the KERL... isolation... may be prohibitively expensive. Consequently, ambient verifiability provides asymmetry between the attacker and the defender in favor of the defender." (KERI spec, L1884)

The observer-enabled properties are themselves conditional: **O1** Duplicity Detection (depends A8, A9, A10; "may be delayed or incomplete"); **O2** Evidence-Based Accountability ("Observed duplicity can be proven to third parties"; depends A1, A11; "Proof does not imply remediation"); **O3** Validator-Informed Choice ("Choices may diverge permanently"). (`background.md` §6.5) And what observers do *not* provide is enumerated: "Global convergence / Guaranteed detection / Timely detection / Automatic resolution / Enforcement against controllers or witnesses." The one-line summary is doctrine: "**Observers increase *visibility*, not *control*.**" (`background.md` §6.6)

The papers add a governance-flavored distinction that reviewers coming from the browser/CA world need: a watcher makes an *observation*, not an *enforcement* decision. "A browser vendor that distrusts a CA is making an enforcement decision -- exercising judgment with immediate global consequences... A KERI watcher that detects duplicity is making an observation -- surfacing cryptographic evidence that the relying party confirms independently." (`wtbo.md` §4) "Watchers cannot unilaterally 'distrust' an identifier the way a browser root program can distrust a CA. The worst a dishonest watcher can do is stay silent about duplicity it has seen -- a risk addressed by running your own watcher or consulting multiple independent ones." (`wtbo.md` §4) The recommended default is the *local-resolver* pattern: a watcher "can be (and by default usually is) as simple as an in-process component in a verifier's own software stack that queries multiple witnesses directly before reporting any result." (`wtbo.md` §5)

A role-boundary invariant guards against a subtle confusion: **a witness is NOT a watcher.** A witness may serve a delegate's KEL without the delegator's seal or KEL; a validator "must independently obtain the delegator's KEL (OOBI) before validating." (`30-invariants.md` D3; `31-landmines.md` L4) Code that assumes a witness performs watcher functionality will fail.

**Contested / immature -- flag for reviewers.** The observer layer is where the corpus's own honest criticism lands. It is the *only* residual that survives objective-function realignment across the six Del Giudice claims, and it is a deployment-maturity gap, not a protocol flaw. "A KERI deployment without robust watchers and governance is not a secure system." (`cr-ad-trust.md` §5) The CISO reading is blunt: treat "observer/watcher infrastructure as a first-class security control, not an optional add-on," and expect early-deployment detection to be "delayed rather than immediate," "probabilistic rather than deterministic," "coverage-dependent," and "action-dependent" on governance. (dg-c0X §7 non-normative notes) Real failure modes named: correlated witness failure (same cloud provider), weak thresholds chosen for convenience, siloed or lazy watchers, and alert fatigue. (`cr-ad-trust.md` §5; dg-c0X §7)

## 10. Local vs global validity

The deepest departure from blockchain intuition is that KERI does not promise validators will ever agree. Validity is *local to a validator* and observer-dependent. The corpus disambiguates four notions of convergence, and a reviewer must keep them apart:

- **Local Consistency** -- "Its accepted KERL is internally consistent / No conflicts observed within its view." "Local consistency is always achievable." (`background.md` §7.4.1)
- **Global Convergence** -- "All validators accept the same KERL for an identifier." "**KERI does not guarantee global convergence.**" (`background.md` §7.4.2)
- **Eventual Awareness** -- "Conflicts become observable to some validators over time." "This is possible, not guaranteed." (`background.md` §7.4.3)
- **Permanent Divergence** -- "Validators accept different KERLs indefinitely." "**Permanent divergence is an allowed outcome.**" (`background.md` §7.4.4)

Safety and liveness are correspondingly reframed. "Safety in KERI means: Validators do not accept *invalid* histories." (`background.md` §7.5.1) It does **not** mean validators agree on a single history. "Liveness in KERI means: Honest controllers can continue to append events." (`background.md` §7.5.2) It does **not** mean prompt observation or conflict resolution. Validators are autonomous: they "do not share state by default," "May have disjoint trust relationships," and "May never interact directly." (`background.md` §7.1)

The spec states the local-validity rule as a validator obligation: "An honest validator MUST trust when there is no evidence of duplicity and MUST NOT trust when there is any evidence of duplicity unless and until the duplicity has been reconciled." (KERI spec, L53) Whether a validator treats its own first-seen copy as authoritative is "at the validator's discretion." (KERI spec, L1842) The reconciliation model treats the KEL as a DAG whose undisputed path is the *trunk*; if validators cannot universally find the same undisputed path, the KEL is *irreconcilable* and is not trusted. (KERI spec, §Reconciliation, L1788-1790)

It is essential to note that permanent divergence is a *protocol-level property*, not merely a deployment artifact: it is "permitted *even with observers present*." (`dg-c03-claude.md` §3) Observers can make divergence *visible*; they cannot make it *converge*. This is the fact that a blockchain-trained reviewer finds most surprising and the corpus most insistent upon.

## 11. The Del Giudice claim bindings (DG-C01..C06)

The 2025 ETH Zurich Master's thesis by Del Giudice, *A Security Analysis of KERI*, is "the most rigorous formal analysis of KERI's consensus mechanics" but "not peer-reviewed research." (`cr-ad-trust.md` §5) Its six normalized claims are the canonical checklist for what KERI does and does not guarantee, and each has been adjudicated against KERI's stated objective function. The meta-pattern is the most useful single takeaway: every apparent weakness resolves into one of three buckets -- a deliberate non-goal, a proof-scope limitation, or a deployment/ecosystem-maturity gap -- and the only genuinely surviving residuals are about observer-infrastructure immaturity, never about the protocol. (`background.md` §8.2, meta-pattern)

- **DG-C01 -- KAWA provides fork-resistance for honest controllers (under strong assumptions).** KAWA gives validity, consistency, and reliability for honest controllers during establishment events, under BFT-style quorum sizing and bounded faults. Assumptions: A1, A2, A3, A4, A7. Verdict: **faithful within scope; a positive claim, not a criticism.** Risks are all reader-side over-generalization: assuming it extends to all deployments, under-appreciating the detection layer, or assuming BFT thresholds are mandatory. (`dg-c01-claude.md`)

- **DG-C02 -- KERI does not prevent duplicity by malicious controllers.** At best duplicity is detectable via evidence propagation; neither prevention nor timely detection is guaranteed. Verdict: **faithful to the mechanics but misaligned with KERI's objectives** -- it "criticizes KERI for lacking a capability... that is **explicitly outside KERI's design goals**," treating a non-goal as a failure and making KERI "appear **less secure than it is**." The one *legitimate* residual that survives realignment: detection is delegated to an *optional* observer layer, which is "a critique of *deployment readiness and ecosystem maturity*, not of KERI's protocol design." (`dg-c02-claude.md` §2, §5, §6) The thesis's own honest phrasing: "We don't have any guarantees however on if and when this duplicity will be exposed." (Ch.4 §4.1 p.38)

- **DG-C03 -- Validators may accept conflicting KERLs indefinitely.** Different validators may accept different valid KERLs, and divergence may persist indefinitely with no protocol-level resolution -> "not duplicity-resistant but only duplicity-evident." Assumptions: A12, A13. Verdict: the **descriptive portion is faithful** (permanent divergence is a permitted outcome, §7.4.4); the evaluative "only" risks importing an invulnerability expectation. "**No criticism survives objective-function alignment**" when KERI is evaluated as survivability-oriented. Note the sharp point: divergence is a protocol property, "permitted *even with observers present*." (`dg-c03-claude.md` §2, §3, §6)

- **DG-C04 -- Recovery rotation blocks proof of a single authoritative KERL.** Under recovery rotation, correct witnesses may sign both an attacker's fraudulent event and the legitimate owner's recovery event at the same sequence number, "because KERI prioritizes the ability to reclaim a compromised identity over strict uniqueness of history at every moment." (`cr-ad-trust.md` §5) The consequence: "validators may see multiple mathematically valid KELs for the same identifier, and no cryptographic mechanism alone can determine which is legitimate." (`cr-ad-trust.md` §5) Assumptions: none beyond the base model -- the fork occurs *even when all actors behave correctly*. Verdict: technically correct; it "buys real-world recoverability at the cost of formal elegance in edge cases." Treating "inability to prove uniqueness" as insecurity is a category error, because "KERI's security model is **evidence-based** rather than **proof-based**"; formal uniqueness is unprovable, but "**duplicity remains detectable**." (`dg-c04-claude.md` §5.1) The alternative would be "an identity you cannot recover from compromise without involving a trusted third party -- which brings back exactly the administrative dependency KERI was designed to escape." (`cr-ad-trust.md` §5)

- **DG-C05 -- Practical mitigation requires governance and monitoring.** Effective mitigation "likely requires governance, monitoring, or centralized archival services (e.g., a 'super watcher'), which introduce additional trust assumptions." Assumptions: A8-A11. Verdict: **faithful and survivability-aligned** -- "not a failure of KERI's design but an acknowledgment that survivability properties emerge from **system composition** (protocol + observers + governance), not protocol alone." Caveat: "additional trust assumptions" is better read as operational dependencies and incentive/coverage assumptions -- except the super-watcher case, which *is* genuine trust centralization. (`dg-c05-claude.md` §2, §5, §6) The GLEIF super-watcher is the live example: "This works, but it reintroduces a centralized trust dependency that KERI was designed to avoid." (`cr-ad-trust.md` §5)

- **DG-C06 -- Strong security results depend on narrow scope plus strong assumptions.** The strongest proofs hold only for honest controllers, bounded witness faults, reliable communication, and uncompromised pre-committed rotation keys; malicious-controller behavior breaks termination/liveness *in the proofs*. Verdict: **faithful as a statement about the proofs' scope**, but misleading if read as "KERI requires honest controllers." The key correction: observer mechanisms are "explicitly excluded from Del Giudice's formal analysis" (`background.md` §8.3) yet are "central to KERI's security model." Malicious controllers "trigger detection, not failure," and honest-controller is "a **proof technique assumption**... not a KERI deployment assumption." (`dg-c06-claude.md` §3, §4, §5)

## 12. What is established, what is contested, and the load-bearing assumptions

**Established (uncontested within the corpus, stated identically across security analysis, papers, spec, and the reference-implementation knowledge base):** the survivability objective function; detection-over-prevention; duplicity-evidence as the characteristic property; the zero-trust/malicious-controller stance and its status as an explicit non-goal to *prevent* a fully malicious controller; the pre-rotation firewall and the survivable-vs-catastrophic asymmetry between current-key and next-key compromise; KAWA's provided properties P1-P4 and its non-properties P5-P8; the local/observer-dependent nature of validity and the permissibility of permanent divergence; and the observers-increase-visibility-not-control boundary.

**Contested or genuinely limiting (the residuals that survive scrutiny):** exactly one class of criticism survives objective-function alignment, and it is not about the protocol -- it is that KERI's *detection* depends on an *optional, still-immature* observer/watcher/governance layer (the surviving residual of DG-C02, and the whole of DG-C05). "A KERI deployment without robust watchers and governance is not a secure system." (`cr-ad-trust.md` §5) The super-watcher pattern relieves coverage gaps but reintroduces genuine centralization (`cr-ad-trust.md` §5; DG-C05). Separately, the formal literature is thin: the strongest analysis is a non-peer-reviewed Master's thesis, and it explicitly excludes the observer layer that KERI's security model most depends on. (`wtbo.md` §5; `background.md` §8.3) None of this is a design defect; the corpus is candid that these are "constraints of *immaturity*, not of *architecture*." (`cr-ad-trust.md` §6)

**A companion problem about evidence standards, which the corpus raised against itself this month.** The thinness of the formal literature has a second edge worth naming here, because an author in this corpus named it about his own work. A paper of his once described the review behind its central estimate as having been submitted "to two independent adversarial expert reviews, one in vision science, one in security usability" (`m-glance.md` L66-67 @`32d2d43`). The current revision retracts that: those passes were "conducted by AI models prompted to argue from a named lens, not by human domain experts", and "They are a structured way to attack one's own assumptions, not independent validation" (`m-glance.md` §1, L71-74 @`181569b64`). A sibling paper made the same move in the same window, restating its own status downward to "*theoretically motivated, adversarially self-reviewed, and empirically untested*" (`amp-diff.md` §6.3, L405 @`181569b64`). Neither paper is about KERI and neither is cited anywhere else in this chapter; the finding is methodological, and it lands squarely on how this chapter's own material was produced. A structured adversarial pass -- a persona panel, a red-team prompt, a lens-driven critique -- attacks your own assumptions, and it produces exactly the sort of confident, well-organized output that reads like peer review and is not. Its evidentiary weight sits below a non-peer-reviewed Master's thesis, not beside it. That bears directly on the six per-claim adjudications in §11: their filenames (`dg-c01-claude.md` and siblings) mark them as model-authored, which makes them a structured self-attack on the thesis rather than an independent check of it, and they should be read and cited as such. The DG verdicts remain the most careful reading of Del Giudice this corpus has. They are not a second opinion.

**Load-bearing assumptions a reviewer must never grant for free:** A3 (honest controller -- a proof convenience, not a field premise); A5 (observer presence -- optional and MUST NOT be silently assumed); A7 (threshold discipline M > f -- M = 1 is trivially breakable); and the entire A8-A11 quartet (observer coverage, diligence, information flow, evidence integrity -- *none* enforced by KERI). Any claim that duplicity *will* be detected, *promptly* detected, or *automatically* resolved is smuggling one or more of these in, and violates the P5-P8 non-properties. Any claim of a single global truth for an identifier contradicts the local-validity model outright.

## 13. Where KERI corrects the outsider's prior

Several review reflexes are, in the corpus's own terms, category errors. Named explicitly so a reviewer can catch them in their own reasoning:

- **"Security means invulnerability; prevent all forks."** Category error. KERI is survivability-oriented; forks by malicious controllers are anticipated disturbances, and criticizing the lack of prevention is "criticizing a smoke detector for not preventing fires." (`background.md` §4.5.1; `dg-c02-claude.md` §5)
- **"You need a blockchain / global consensus / global total ordering."** Explicit non-goal. (`background.md` §1.3) Key events are idempotent authorization operations, so local ordering suffices; the event is *already recorded* on its microledger, and KAWA does post-hoc fork detection, not consensus on inclusion. (KERI spec §KAWA insight, L1836; DECENTRALIZATION-PURITY-STEERING)
- **"A threshold of witnesses equals global safety."** No. "Agreement thresholds do not imply global safety." (`background.md` §4.7) KAWA gives local durability; global detectability needs observers.
- **"All validators converge on one truth (finality)."** No. Permanent divergence is allowed; safety does not mean agreement on a single history; validators may never interact. (`background.md` §7.4, §7.5, §7.1)
- **"Detection is inferior to prevention."** A smuggled invulnerability premise; the argument must justify *why* prevention is required given KERI's threat model, and it never does. (`dg-c02-claude.md` §5)
- **"If a formal proof assumes an honest controller, the system is insecure against dishonest ones."** The honest-controller premise is a proof simplification; dishonesty triggers detection, not failure. (`dg-c06-claude.md` §3, §5)
- **"Unprovable uniqueness means broken."** KERI is evidence-based, not proof-based; recovery rotation deliberately trades provable uniqueness for real-world recoverability without a trusted third party. (`dg-c04-claude.md`; `cr-ad-trust.md` §5)
- **"The root of trust must be an authority (CA/administrator)."** KERI's root is the identifier's own key-event history, replayable offline "without querying any authority." Witnesses "make no assertion about identity." (`cr-ad-trust.md` §3-§4; `00-lens.md` claim 1)

The method-level tell that ties these together: the analysis template *forces* the analyst to name the objective function first and to restate any claim verbatim, precisely because outsider critiques smuggle in unstated invulnerability goals. "you MUST restate the claim verbatim... If you cannot do so, stop and ask." (`standard-analysis.md` §0) A reviewer who cannot state which objective function a criticism assumes has not yet earned the right to make it.

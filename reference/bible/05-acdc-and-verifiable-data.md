# ACDC & Verifiable Data

**Thesis.** An Authentic Chained Data Container (ACDC) is not a "credential format" in the W3C-VC or SD-JWT sense; it is a fragment of a globally distributed, cryptographically self-addressed property graph whose every node commits to its own content by a SAID, whose edges are typed and operator-bearing, and whose authority is bound not to a signing key but to an issuer's evolving key state anchored in a KEL or TEL. This chapter sets out the machinery that makes that claim precise: SAIDs and the single canonical "most compact form" digest algorithm; the mutually exclusive attribute (`a`) and aggregate (`A`) sections and the partial-vs-selective disclosure split they encode; the edge section and the unary/m-ary operator families that turn a bag of credentials into a reasoned graph of authority; the Ricardian-contract rule section; the graduated-disclosure ladder from compact through metadata, partial, nested-partial, selective, and full disclosure; contractually-protected disclosure (chain-link confidentiality and contingent disclosure); TEL-based registries and the observer/registrar split that refuses phone-home revocation; the IPEX exchange state machine; and the applied corpus (SEDI, GCD, vLEI, the Dossier) that shows what real schemas actually enforce. Throughout, the reference implementation (`keripy`) is treated as ground truth against the spec's prose, and where the two diverge — most sharply in the edge-operator family — that gap is stated plainly rather than papered over.

Adversarial reviewers should carry one framing into every section: ACDC's design goal is *not* data privacy per se but "the more general goal of protection from the unpermissioned exploitation of data" (ACDC spec §Disclosure Mechanisms, L1659). Privacy mechanisms are means, not ends. A critique that measures ACDC against a pure-privacy or pure-ZK yardstick is measuring the wrong thing.

## 1. What an ACDC fundamentally is

An ACDC is modeled abstractly as an **ordered nested field map** (`key:value`), serializable in JSON, CBOR, MGPK, or CESR, in which every field map MUST contain a SAID field — making each map a **SAD (Self-Addressed Data structure)**: "One important feature of the fields maps used by ACDC is that they all MUST include a field with a SAID" (ACDC spec §ACDC Message Fields, L2982). The container carries no top-level type field, because "the Schema, `s`, field itself is the type field" (§Type-is-schema, L182); this is the recurring "type-is-schema" principle, whose corollary is that "type information is metadata, not data" (L182). Insertion-ordered field maps MUST be used for canonical serialization — never lexicographic (§Ordered Nested Field Maps, L10) — which is the design's direct answer to the JSON-canonicalization and JSON-LD term-redefinition fragilities that let a signed VC's meaning shift without breaking its signature (papers, `keri-primer.md §4.1`, citing W3C vc-data-integrity issue #272).

A set of ACDCs "as nodes connected by edges forms a labeled property graph," each node "universally uniquely identified by the SAID of its ACDC," yielding "securely attributed fragments of a globally distributed property graph... a global verifiable knowledge graph that crosses trust domains" (§ACDCs as secure graph fragments, L1060-1068). No shared or trusted relationship between controllers and verifiers is required (§AID Fields, L95). In Hardman's own framing, ACDC is "not just a format — they're a *methodology* for creating verifiable evidence" (`x509-prob.md`), and the container is "not a mutable document; it is a crystallized fact" (`keri-primer.md §3.2`).

**Top-level field order (MUST):** `[v, t, d, u, i, rd, s, a, A, e, r]` (§Field Ordering, L32); **required fields:** `[v, d, i, s]` (§Required Fields, L36). The hardest structural invariant: an ACDC MUST NOT have both a non-empty `a` and a non-empty `A` value (L106, L110) — attribute and aggregate sections are mutually exclusive.

## 2. SAIDs and SAIDification

A SAID is "a special type of cryptographic digest of its encapsulating field map (block)" (§SAID Fields, L77). Its load-bearing property is **commitment equivalence**: "A digital signature on a SAID makes a verifiable cryptographic non-repudiable commitment that is equivalent to a commitment on the full serialization of the associated block from which the SAID was derived" (L81), requiring collision resistance and ~128-bit strength. Because a SAID may itself digest a block containing further SAIDs, the commitment is **hierarchical** — a single top-level commitment binds an entire tree.

The derivation mechanism is the **dummy-fill-then-digest** algorithm, and the reference implementation is the ground truth here. `Saider._derive` (`keripy core/coring.py:4058-4096`): copy the `sad`, fill the id field with the dummy character `#` to the exact full size of the digest (`sad[label] = Dummy * Matter.Sizes[code].fs`, `coring.py:4085`; the dummy "must not be a valid Base64 char", `coring.py:3949`), recompute the version-string size over the dummied serialization if versioned, digest the serialization, then inject the result back into the field. Verification (`Saider.verify`, `coring.py:4117-4160`) re-derives with dummy fill and compares `qb64b`; any exception returns `False` — fail-closed. So the SAID is "a tamper-evident commitment to exact serialized content at exact size"; change any non-ignored byte and verification fails. This is exactly the "44 `#` chars for Base64 256-bit, hash, overwrite" story in the papers (`keri-primer.md §3.2`), made mechanical.

Two subtleties matter for reviewers. First, **there MUST be one and only one unambiguous way to compute the SAID** of a compactifiable section: the **most compact form** SAID, computed by depth-first search — SAID the expanded leaves, compact them, ascend computing enclosing-block SAIDs up to the trunk (§Most compact form SAID, L130-151). keripy invariant J3 states this at the credential layer: "An ACDC SAID is computed over its most-compact form, not its uncompacted serialization, so the SAID is stable across compaction/disclosure" (keripy-knowledge `30-invariants.md` J3; `vc/proving.py`). This stability is what makes graduated disclosure verifiable: "enables verification of portions of a set of nested compactifiable subblocks against their SAIDs without requiring that the whole tree be exposed. This is essential to Graduated Disclosure" (§Most compact form SAID, L136).

Second, the **schema SAID uses the field label `$id`**, not `d` — a repurposed JSON Schema keyword whose value MUST be the bare schema SAID, not a URI (§Schema ID Field Label, L186-210). In the applied corpus this is stated as a hard rule: "`$id` is a SAID... Edit content -> the SAID changes... Never invent or hand-patch a SAID" (`bakobo/schema/KNOWLEDGE_TRANSFER.md`).

**The UUID `u` field is a blinding factor, not decoration.** Without it, "an adversary may be able to reconstruct the block contents merely from the SAID... and the Schema... using a rainbow or dictionary attack," because the power set of schema-allowed field values may be far smaller than the digest strength (§UUID Fields, L89). Sufficient entropy in `u` "ensures that the cardinality of the power set allowed by the schema is at least as great as the entropy of the SAID digest algorithm." This is the crisp correction of a common outsider assumption: **compactness is not privacy.** A public (no-`u`) compact ACDC is still rainbow-attackable — "It only provides compactness, not privacy" (§Targeted Public-attribute, L501). The presence or absence of `u` at top level is precisely what separates a **public** ACDC (no `u`) from a **private** one (`u` with entropy), and an *empty* `u` marks the **metadata** variant (§ACDC Variants, L120-174).

## 3. Static, immutable schema

Schemas MUST be completely self-contained and statically fixed: "dynamic Schema references or dynamic Schema generation mechanisms MUST NOT be used" (§Static Schema, L194). The spec names two attacks this prevents. The **schema revocation attack**: an attacker who can mutate a dynamic schema resource makes validation fail on every ACDC using it, "effectively revok[ing] all the ACDCs that use that dynamic Schema reference" (L196). The **semantic malleability attack** (a transaction-malleability variant): an attacker "shift[s] the semantics of the dynamic Schema" so the ACDC still validates but downstream behavior changes (L198). Prevention: "all Schemas MUST be static, i.e., Schemas MUST be SADs and therefore verifiable against their SAIDs" (L200). The `$schema` dialect URI "is simply an identifier"; dereferencing it for validation code "would be an attack vector" — the Validator MUST control the tooling dialect (JSON Schema 2020-12) (§Schema dialect, L226). This is the ACDC answer to the JSON-LD `@context` link-rot and term-redefinition problems (papers `acdc-vc-diff.md §Loss 6`).

A **composable schema** uses `oneOf` to bundle compact and expanded variants: "a verifiable bundle of metadata (composed) about content that can then be verifiably unbundled (decomposed) later. The Issuer makes a single verifiable commitment to the bundle" (L246). The Validator uses the composed base schema before disclosure and the decomposed schema (compact option removed) after, to *force* full disclosure. Two schema "versions" coexist and must not be confused: the `$id` SAID is "the normative determiner" used in validation; the semver `version` field is "informative" and is NOT used in validation (§Schema Versioning, L230). Availability is orthogonal to security: "Unavailable does not mean insecure or unverifiable. ACDCs MUST be verifiable when available" (§Schema Availability, L242).

## 4. Attribute section `a` vs aggregate section `A`

The two disclosable payload sections encode two different disclosure mechanisms, and choosing between them is the single most consequential schema-design decision.

The **attribute section `a`** is a labeled field map. Reserved nested labels are `d, u, i, dt` (§Attribute Section, L260). Its presence-of-`i` distinguishes **Targeted** (an Issuee AID is present — "a provably controllable identifier that is the Target," making the ACDC usable as a Verifiable Credential, L312) from **Untargeted** ("issued 'to whom it may concern'", verifiable authorship only, no counterparty, no delegation, L330). Crucially the Issuee `i` sits *nested in* `a`, not at top level, so it is "partially disclosable / correlatable only after Chain-Link Confidentiality [is] accepted" (L316). Because field labels are public (they live in the schema), the attribute section supports **partial disclosure** — each nested block carries its own `d` + `u`, and a withheld block travels as its bare SAID (`docs/choosing-attribute-vs-aggregate.md`).

The **aggregate section `A`** (capital) is categorically different: "its compact value is an aggregate / AGID (Aggregate ID), NOT a SAID" (§Aggregate Section, L661). It is an ordered array of blinded blocks, each with its own `d` + `u`; "the block labels live *inside* each block, so they're blinded; array position is arbitrary" (`docs/choosing-attribute-vs-aggregate.md`). This gives **selective disclosure**: field labels *and* positions are hidden, so `anyOf` ordering "is not correlated to the actual order" and cannot leak information by position (§Aggregate Section, L694). The AGID is computed as `AGID = H(C(a_i for i in {0..N}))`: the zeroth list element is dummied with `#` chars, the remaining N elements are block SAIDs, the list is serialized and digested (Blake3-256), and the digest replaces the dummy (L712-747). Its security rests on "sufficient cryptographic entropy of the blinding factors, collision resistance of the digests, and unforgeability of the non-repudiable digital commitments," and the digest-of-ordered-concatenation is "not subject to a birthday attack" (L688, L728). An inclusion proof discloses the detailed block at index j, the full list, the compact ACDC, and the issuance seal; the Disclosee recomputes the block, checks membership, recomputes the AGID, and confirms the KEL seal (L734-750).

**The deciding question**, from the applied corpus, is exact: "Does hiding the field labels or positions buy you anything?" (`choosing-attribute-vs-aggregate.md`). The aggregate provides label/position blinding, valuable in exactly two cases — the presence/identity of a field is itself sensitive, or the field set is variable/homogeneous. Otherwise the aggregate is "pure cost, because you lose label-based pathing": graduated-disclosure negotiation (apply/offer path lists via keripy's `Pather`) "wants to name a field as `a/residenceState`. In an aggregate you can't." One further caveat worth quoting: "Attribute-form partial disclosure forces the issuee (`a.i`) and the `a`-section metadata to be revealed on any disclosure; an aggregate can disclose one element with no issuee." SEDI's `sedi-age` is the aggregate done right — a homogeneous boolean vector `ageOver13/16/18/21/55/65` with element 0 as the AGID, "exactly the ISO mDL `age_over_NN` model... a poor man's sparse Merkle tree" that satisfies a statutory "prove a minimum age without disclosing age or birth date" duty (`sedi-age/index.md`).

The distinction from partial disclosure is subtle and load-bearing: a partially-disclosed block, when fully disclosed, "exposes, at the very least, the labels of other fields in its enclosing block," whereas a selectively-disclosed block "does not expose any information about other yet-to-be-exposed fields, including their labels" (L2733). Selective disclosure is "separating a 'stew' of 'ingredients' into its constituent ingredients without correlating them via the stew."

## 5. Edges, chaining, and the operator families

Edges connect ACDCs into a **DAG**: the near node is the enclosing ACDC, the far node is the ACDC referenced by `n` (a SAID) (§Edge Section, L1172). Reserved edge labels are `[d, u, n, s, o, w]` in fixed order (L1149). An edge's `s` field is an *additional* constraint — the far node MUST validate against both its own schema and the edge's `s` (L1176) — which is what the vLEI ladder uses to const-pin each hop (see §10). This is a genuine capability W3C VCs lack: RDF triples "can't attach properties to edges," forcing reification/"triple bloat," whereas ACDC edges are first-class property-graph objects carrying native `w`/`o`/`s`/`n` (papers `acdc-vc-diff.md §Loss 3`).

**The m-ary operators** (on edge-*groups*, via `o`): `AND` (default; group valid iff all members valid), `OR` (one member valid), `NAND`, `NOR`, `AVG` (arithmetic average of a member property), `WAVG` (weighted average using `w`) (§Edge Section, L1099-1116). Default when `o` is missing is `AND`; validity logic is EGF-dependent.

**The unary operators** (on individual edges, via `o`) are the family reviewers most need to understand, because the gloss and the enforced behavior diverge:

| Op | Spec gloss | Default |
|---|---|---|
| `I2I` | Issuer-To-Issuee: this ACDC's Issuer AID MUST be the Issuee AID of the far node | Yes (targeted far node) |
| `NI2I` | Not-Issuer-To-Issuee: no such requirement | Yes (untargeted far node) |
| `DI2I` | Delegated-Issuer-To-Issuee: this Issuer MUST be the Issuee AID *or a delegated AID of* the Issuee | No |
| `NOT` | logical NOT — inverts far node validity | No |

The spec's default inference: a targeted far node appends `I2I`, an untargeted far node appends `NI2I` (L1197); on a conflict list "the latest Operator... in the list takes precedence" (L1186). A chain of I2I-linked targeted ACDCs "can be used to provide a chain-of-authority... a delegation chain for authorization" (L1203).

### 5.1 verifying.py ground truth — gloss versus enforced behavior

This is where an adversarial reviewer earns their keep, and where the reference implementation must be cited against the prose. `Verifier.verifyChain(nodeSaid, op, issuer)` in `keripy vdr/verifying.py:336-380` is what *actually* enforces edge semantics, and it enforces less than the prose implies:

- **Only three operators are recognized**: `['I2I', 'DI2I', 'NI2I']` (`verifying.py:354`). `NOT`, `AND`, `OR`, and the arithmetic operators have no code path here.
- **When `o` is absent, the operator is silently inferred**: `op = 'I2I' if 'i' in creder.attrib else 'NI2I'` (`verifying.py:355`). The "explicit operator" story is softer than the prose suggests — a missing operator defaults from the mere presence of a subject `i`.
- **I2I is enforced as plain AID string equality**: `if op == 'I2I' and issuer != creder.attrib['i']: return None` (`verifying.py:365-366`). "That is the entirety of the I2I check — an equality of qb64 AID strings. No chain-of-authority reasoning beyond it." The multi-hop "chain of authority" is an emergent property of the recursive edge walk, not of any operator that re-derives delegated authority.
- **DI2I is NOT IMPLEMENTED**: `if op == "DI2I": raise NotImplementedError()` (`verifying.py:368-369`). Every prose description of delegated-issuer-to-issuee edge behavior — including the Dossier reference's operators table (`operators-ref`) — is, in this verifier, "a gloss with no machine behavior... it will crash if exercised."
- **NI2I** skips the issuer/issuee binding entirely; it "only requires the node credential exists and is in issued state" — a non-authority reference.
- **Chain revocation propagates**: a revoked far node raises `RevokedChainError` (`verifying.py:178-180`) — but note the *root* credential is still saved even when revoked (see §8).

Two open keripy landmines widen this gap. **L16**: "ACDC edge operator semantics... are not enforced" — the m-ary `&&`/`||` group operators are read but unused (keripy-knowledge `31-landmines.md` L16, twin #885). **L17**: "Edge/provenance DAG acyclicity... not validated" and NI2I chaining "isn't fully validated" (L17, twin #1040). A reviewer evaluating an edge-heavy design proposal must treat the operator gloss as *specified intent*, not *deployed enforcement*, on the keripy 2.0-dev line. This is the single most important prose-versus-machine divergence in the ACDC layer.

### 5.2 The same-holder binding pattern

The applied corpus supplies the sharpest doctrinal statement of what I2I *means* in practice: "I2I holds only when the issuer of this presentation is the issuee of the credential the edge points to... it is what distinguishes 'Alice presenting *her own* credentials' from 'two credentials that merely share an AID,' which anyone could assemble" (`sedi-present-age-portrait/index.md`). In a holder-issued presentation the holder is the issuer and is the issuee of both source credentials, so I2I edges to both are the same-holder proof; a non-I2I operator is rejected (`const "I2I"`). The corollary is a chaining trap worth internalizing: "don't force an edge whose operator semantics you can't actually satisfy; bind at presentation instead." `sedi-age` deliberately carries *no* edge to `sedi-id`, because an I2I edge would hold only if the holder self-issued the age attestation (`sedi-age/index.md`).

## 6. Rules and Ricardian contracts

The rule section `r` is a **Ricardian Contract (RC)**: "both human and machine-readable and referenceable by a cryptographic digest," with the section's top-level SAID `d` providing the digest that "supports the bow-tie model of RC" (§Rule Section, L1239). Rules are terminal (MUST have a legal `l` field); rule-groups are intermediate and may nest; reserved labels are `[d, u, l]` and a Rule MUST NOT have other fields (L1265-1300). A rule with both `d` and high-entropy `u` is private — protected "from discovery via a rainbow table attack merely from its SAID and subschema" (L1313). The spec's worked examples are `warrantyDisclaimer`/`liabilityDisclaimer` and a `permittedUse` clause ("MAY only use this ACDC for non-commercial purposes", L1512).

The applied GCD corpus turns the rule section into a governance surface with five required disclaimers, each quotable doctrine: `noRoleSemanticsWithoutGfw` ("the role field has no enforceable semantics unless the gfw field is also defined"), `issuerNotResponsibleOutsideConstraints`, `noConstraintOutsideConstraints` ("enforceable constraints exist only inside the constraints container... an unrecognized key inside the constraints container MUST be treated as fail-closed"), `useStdIfPossible`, and `onlyDelegateHeldAuthority` ("Issuers agree to only delegate authority that they reasonably believe they hold") (`gcd/rules.json`). The `gfw` field names a supplemental rules block and carries a hard verifier obligation: "When defined, a verifier MUST NOT impute a delegator's approval of delegated authority to a delegate without understanding and enforcing the rules of these extra rules," and "The act of issuing or receiving a GCD credential constitutes binding acceptance of the rules" (`gcd/index.md §Governance`). This binding-by-participation pattern recurs verbatim in the SEDI governance framework's chain-link-confidentiality clause.

**Load-bearing caveat for reviewers:** rules are *disclosure and accountability*, and in keripy the disclosure/rule layer is largely *not machine-enforced* by the credential `Verifier`. The keripy crosswalk flags this explicitly: "disclosure rules not enforced in `Verifier`" (keripy-knowledge `20-crosswalk.md`). A rule clause is a commitment a human or an application-layer policy engine must honor; it is not a predicate the reference verifier evaluates. GCD's own doctrine embraces this — duties "live in `r`, not in `a.constraints`" precisely because "a stranger-verifier does not gate on them" (`gcd` schema duties.description).

## 7. The graduated-disclosure ladder

"Graduated Disclosure" is recursive least disclosure: "disclose enough to enable more disclosure, which in turn may enable even more disclosure" (§Graduated Disclosure, L1747). (Note the deliberate vocabulary choice: "selective disclosure" is avoided as a top-level term because it "has a long and unpleasant history in financial regulation," per `sdjwt-acdc.md §1`.) The ladder's rungs (L1749-1774):

1. **Compact Disclosure** — a SAID commitment via `oneOf` compact/full; compactness only, *not* privacy.
2. **Metadata Disclosure** — the metadata ACDC (empty `u`) lets a Discloser commit to metadata (Issuer, Schema, Edges, Rules) of a yet-to-be-disclosed private ACDC "without providing any point of correlation to the actual top-level SAID." Critically, "When metadata ACDC is disclosed, only Discloser commitments attach, NOT Issuer commitments" — so the Issuer's commitments do not become a correlation point until after the Disclosee agrees to the Rules (§Metadata ACDC, L168-172).
3. **Partial Disclosure** — SAID + salty nonce; SAID + schema is not enough to discover content.
4. **Nested Partial Disclosure** — each nested block carries its own `d` + `u`, with per-level `oneOf`, so branches disclose at different nesting depths (L633).
5. **Full Disclosure** — no hiding.
6. **Selective Disclosure** — `anyOf` + `oneOf`, order-independent, membership via the SAID set (the aggregate mechanism of §4).
7. **Bulk-issued Instance Disclosure** — many non-correlatable instances (see §9).

"All the Graduated Disclosure mechanisms MAY be used in combination" (L1775). The keripy reference `tests/acdc/test_clc_disclosure.py` is the executable exemplar cited by the SEDI corpus (`sedi-present-age-portrait/index.md`).

An important disambiguation for reviewers who arrive from the Merkle-tree / transparency-log world: ACDC graduated disclosure and Merkle Tree Certificate inclusion proofs share hash vocabulary but discharge different obligations. ACDC's proof obligation is *semantic* — "that a particular view corresponds to a specific underlying claim" — motivated by correlation-minimization; MTC's is *historical/membership* — "is this object a member of a committed set" — motivated by efficiency. "A Merkle inclusion proof establishes placement in a history. A SAID establishes identity of content" (`acdc-and-mtc.md §3.2`). They are orthogonal, not competitive.

## 8. Contractually-protected disclosure

Two mechanisms attach obligations to disclosed data (§L1783-1792):

**Chain-Link Confidentiality (CLC)**: the offer is a partial disclosure of metadata plus terms; full disclosure follows only after the Disclosee agrees — "permissioned disclosure" in which "the disclosed data has 'strings attached.'" CLC *chains Disclosees* (distinct from the edge-chaining of ACDCs): "each Disclosee... in turn is the Discloser to the next," and terms-of-use MUST propagate, imposing "conditions and limitations on the further disclosure and/or use." The SEDI governance framework operationalizes this as the `chainLinkConfidentiality` rule: the verifier MUST NOT "assimilate, aggregate, correlate, sell, or otherwise combine disclosed attributes... for any purpose beyond the disclosed purpose," and this "binds each downstream recipient reached through the edge section of a presentation" (`sedi-id/rules.json`).

**Contingent Disclosure**: an obligation in the Rules to disclose when a contingency is met (e.g. breach); the responsible party may be the Discloser or an escrow agent, and the rule references a private ACDC. This enables **latent accountability**: "Recourse via Full Disclosure of PII is latent... but never realized until the conditions of the contingency is satisfied," limiting PII exposure to a "just-in-time, need-to-know basis" (L1792).

The doctrine that governs when to reach for expensive cryptographic unlinkability instead is blunt: cryptographically-provable correlation resistance is **unlinkability**, but "this is a weak form... as it does not prevent statistical correlation from contextual information." "There is no cryptographic mechanism that precludes statistical correlation among a set of colluding Verifiers" (§Bulk-issued, L2785). Consequently, advanced cryptographic unlinkability can be "an exercise in diminishing returns" without contractually-protected disclosure. This is a deliberate, load-bearing design position: ACDC leans on contracts-plus-detection where a ZK-maximalist would spend cryptography.

## 9. Registries and TELs

A **TEL (Transaction Event Log)** is "a hash-chained data structure of sealed transaction events" tracking an ACDC's issuance/revocation/transfer state; TEL events are sealed/anchored in a **KEL** via seals (a seal is an event SAID, optionally plus sequence number). Because they are anchored to a KEL, TELs are "also securely attributable to the KEL's controller" (§TEL Overview, L1918). The persistence property is central: "the verifiability of transaction events in the TEL persists in spite of changes to Key States in the sealing KEL" (L1920). TEL events need not themselves be signed — "the digest in the seal in the KEL is cryptographically equivalent to signing the transaction event itself" (L1946).

Terminology a reviewer should keep straight: ACDCs generically are **Verifiable Containers**; as entitlements they are Verifiable Credentials; a **VCR** (Verifiable Credential Registry) is a type of TEL, which is a form of **VDR** (Verifiable Data Registry); a TEL tracking issuance/revocation is a **Revocation Registry** (§L1948). keripy's `Tever`/`Tevery` implement this, with registry-inception `vcp` and issue/revoke events `iss`/`rev` (backerless) or `bis`/`brv` (backer) (`keripy vdr/eventing.py`).

The **anti-phone-home stance** is a deliberate rejection of OCSP/CRL. A **Registrar** (under the Issuer) maintains and publishes the registry; an **Observer** (under one or more Validators) caches the registry so Validators "validate the state... without exposing a point of validation (PoV)." "It can mask the usage of a given ACDC from the Issuer." Observer-to-Registrar sync happens on state changes, NOT at the point of validation, which "protects against forced validator-to-issuer correlation of ACDC usage, i.e., no forced phone home validation" (§TEL Registrars and TEL Observers, L1693). Race conditions on rare state changes are handled by "timed grace periods on revocations" (L1695). In Hardman's framing, "A registry consulted at verification time is a phone-home in disguise" (`sda.md §7`); the corollary invariant is that "verification must succeed from the credential and key-state alone, and any registry or log is an issuance-time and audit-time convenience, never a verification-time dependency."

**The split is a governance decision, not a caching optimization** — a point the spec never makes but Sam states directly. At KERIcon 2026 he presented Registrar/Observer as a deliberate replay of the witness/watcher structure one layer up: "notice that this models the witness watcher governance structure. So, we learned a lesson. We shouldn't have shared governance. So, we have witnesses that are controlled by controllers, watchers that are controlled by verifiers. We split the verification between two places. We do the same thing with ACDCs" (*The Digital Identity Tradespace*; `raw/14`). The Observer's remit is correspondingly thin — "All our observer is doing is it's keeping track of the registry state" — and it is layered on, not substituted for, KEL assurance: asked why Observers and watchers must be separate components, "they have different purposes... They depend on watchers, but they aren't watchers" (*State of the KERI Suite*). The cleanest one-line distinction in the corpus is from the same Q&A: "watchers watch the KEL... looking for duplicity in your KEL. Observers are watching the state of issuances that are anchored to the KEL... it's a TEL observer." **Caution on provenance:** these are hand-edited auto-captions, `[SAM-DIRECT]` in substance but not verbatim in wording — see `raw/14` §0 before quoting any of it to Sam or into a spec issue.

**The split has a proposed third leg.** Registrar (Issuer-controlled) and Observer (verifier-controlled) leave the Holder as the one party to a transaction running no state infrastructure of its own. A *presentation registry* — an Issuee-controlled blindable state registry that a `grant` must be anchored in — closes that, giving the Issuee the same detect-and-recover capability the Issuer already has against impersonation. It is pre-normative and unimplemented; see `bible/10-presentation-registries.md`, and note that its origin document calls it an *Issuee usage registry* (keripy #1095).

**Bulk updates are a privacy requirement, not a performance one.** The spec mentions "optimized batch synchronization" in a performance register (L1695); the talks make batching load-bearing. "if you update it instantly, then the observer could correlate a change to some registries and not others. But if all the registries, 100% of them update at the same clock time... then there's [synchronization] in the time of update" (*Tradespace*), generalized to a design law in the same talk: "if you want to make it so that it's not statistically correlatable, you have to do it in a bulk or herd privacy protected way." The batch window closes correlation in both directions — backward from a point of validation to the state change that preceded it, forward from a state change to the validations that follow. **The residual weakness is named by Sam himself,** and a reviewer should quote it rather than overclaim: "if an observer is malicious and the issuer is malicious, they can collude... but there's no phone home here. The issuer can't force an observer... There's no technical thing that makes it impossible in this, but what it does is it means that there's no easy way for them to do it" (*Tradespace*). The claim is that collusion is unforced, out-of-band and legally repressible — the same technical-plus-legal posture as the rest of the ACDC privacy story (`bible/06` §three-party exploitation), not a cryptographic impossibility.

**Usage registries — where presenter-side compromise detection actually lives, and the thing most often misattributed to Observers.** Observers cache state; they detect nothing. Detection of a *presenter's* key compromise comes from a second registry controlled by the issuee, in which every presentation is anchored: "if somebody steals my private keys as a presenter, I know that they stole them because the only way that they can gain access is to anchor it in my registry that I control" (*The Future of KERI*). The verifier then checks two registries, the issuer's and the presenter's, "so that both the issuer and the user can detect if either one has been compromised and the verifier knows it." It is opt-in and it costs ephemerality — "Makes the presentation less ephemeral" — and it is the mirror of the issuer-side anti-forgery story at L1687-1689, applied at the other end of the presentation. **Tier this carefully: it is talk-only.** The sole trace in the specification is the `rd` field description listing registry kinds as "Issuance and/or revocation, transfer, retraction, or usage registry" (§Top-Level Fields, L48); there is no usage-registry section, and Sam's own framing of it is a novelty claim ("a new thing that nobody else does").

**Implementation status (2026-09-01): neither component exists.** `keri-foundation/observer` and `keri-foundation/registrar` are public placeholder repos holding a LICENSE and a one-line README; `src/keri/acdc/registraring.py` in keripy is a docstring-only stub Sam created 2026-07-02, and keripy has no observer module at all. What is implemented is the layer beneath — the blindable `bup` update event and `BlindState` structures (`core/structing.py`, `acdc/messaging.py` `blindate()`) and the `RegBaser`/`WebRegBaser` registry stores. Nothing anywhere specifies the Observer wire protocol, how a validator discovers or pools Observers, or how the batch-window-versus-revocation-latency trade should be set. A reviewer should treat Registrar/Observer as **architecturally committed and operationally unbuilt**, and should not accept "the Observer handles that" as an answer to a live design question.

**Binding ACDC state to issuer key state** is the core anti-forgery doctrine, and it is where ACDC most sharply corrects the W3C-VC/SD-JWT prior. ACDCs are "not directly signed by the Issuer... bound to the Issuer's Key State... and the Issuer's Key State is signed. This enables the Key State of the Issuer to change independently of the ACDC state" (§Binding to Key State, L1673). The explicit anti-pattern: in "other verifiable credential schemes, where the credentials are signed directly... a key rotation forces all the credentials signed with a given set of keys to be revoked; otherwise, a key compromise would enable the compromiser to issue... forged [credentials]" (L1675). This is the *retrograde attack* defense (`was.md`): a bare signature "provides no assurance that the private key was used to sign the message at that time" (NIST SP 800-102, via `keri-primer.md §3.3`), so ACDC uses *anchored* rather than *paired* signatures — validity is judged against issuer key state "AS OF its KEL anchor's sequence position, not current key state or wall-clock" (keripy invariant H1, `30-invariants.md`; enforced in `Tever.verifyAnchor`).

**A serious caveat, flagged as a keripy recon hypothesis (L15, HIGH severity, medium confidence).** The credential `Verifier.processCredential` checks registry existence, TEL state, freshness, schema, and chain edges, but recon "found no call to `Tever.verifyAnchor`" in that path — anchor validation appears to happen only when a TEL *event* is ingested by `Tevery`, "not when a credential + proof is verified." If confirmed, "a credential presented with a forged `(seqner, saider)` proof could pass schema + TEL-state checks without the anchor being confirmed against the issuer's actual KEL at that sn — undermining invariant H1" (keripy-knowledge `31-landmines.md` L15). A reviewer relying on anchor-time-of-signing verification must confirm the ingest path actually ran `verifyAnchor` on the events in question; do not assume the credential-verification call re-checks it.

**Revocation is a state, not a deletion.** The keripy verifier explicitly *saves* a revoked credential rather than raising: "Log this and continue instead of the previous exception so we save a revoked credential" (`vdr/verifying.py:129-132`). State is queried from the TEL (`vcState`), not from a central revocation list. This is the direct machine-level contradiction of the "revocation = CRL/blocklist" mindset.

**Blindable state (BLID)** extends registries to private/blinded state: a blinded attribute block `[d, u, td, ts]` whose SAID is computed over a fixed-field concatenation, derived from an Issuer+Discloser shared salt via HD derivation keyed by sequence number, so "an observer cannot correlate state to event updates" (§Blinded State Disclosure, L2043-2135). The Discloser recovers state by brute-forcing the small combination space of possible `td` x `ts` until the BLID matches (only ~6 combinations in the revocation example, L2288). Placeholder decorrelation (publishing empty state before any real ACDC exists) and continued blind updates after revocation "decorrelate the time of revocation" (L2324). Herd privacy can be strengthened with Sparse Merkle Trees amalgamating all seals into one KEL root, giving `O(log N)` inclusion proofs where "a given seal in the KEL... no longer provides a point of correlation to any other transaction event" (L2914).

## 10. IPEX — the issuance and presentation exchange

The ACDC spec's IPEX section is explicitly *non-normative* (§L1795), but the framing is doctrine: "all exchanges (both issuance and presentation) MAY be modeled as the disclosure of information by a Discloser to a Disclosee. The difference between exchange types is the information disclosed, not the mechanism" (L1797). IPEX rides routed KERI `exn` messages, with routes:

| Route | By | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| `apply` | Disclosee | initiate; defines wanted disclosure (schema, label list, aggregate elements, signature) |
| `offer` | Discloser | initiate; proposes acceptable disclosure (Metadata ACDC or SAID, partial disclosure, signature) |
| `agree` | Disclosee | accepts the offer |
| `grant` | Discloser | initiate; discloses the agreed content (Full or Selective Disclosure ACDC, signature) |
| `admit` | Disclosee | confirms receipt |
| `spurn` | either | rejects apply/offer/agree/grant |

The keripy implementation is a strict state machine, not a message log, and this is where prose and code agree cleanly. `Ipexage(apply, offer, agree, grant, admit, spurn)` with a legal-predecessor table `PreviousRoutes` (`vc/protocoling.py:17-23`): `offer` <- `apply`; `agree` <- `offer`; `grant` <- `agree`; `admit` <- `grant`; `spurn` <- any of the four. `IpexHandler.verify` enforces it: `apply` may only start an exchange (empty prior `p`); `offer`/`grant` may start or must reference a legal predecessor; `admit`/`agree`/`spurn` may never start; and there is **no double-response** — "reject if a response to that prior already exists" (`protocoling.py:60-113`). Unmatched routes fail closed. This corrects the outsider assumption that "peer disclosure messages get logged like credentials / like a VC exchange endpoint" — out-of-order or duplicate-response messages are rejected, not merged (`keripy-code` §4).

The commitment mechanics that make IPEX safe across variants: a commitment (signature or KEL-anchored seal) to any variant commits to the shared top-level section fields of *all* variants, because each section value is SAD-or-SAID. "A signature on any variant MAY be used to verify the Issuer's commitment to any other variant... on a top-level section-by-section basis." The variants form a hash tree, and "A commitment to the top-level SAID of the compact version... is equivalent to a commitment to the hash tree root," so the **Issuer MUST provide a signature or seal on the SAID of the most compact form variant** (§Issuer Commitment Rules, L1843). Mutual anchoring — issuer logs "I issued," holder logs "I accepted" — "prevents spam (you can't force a credential into someone's wallet) and provides non-repudiation of receipt" (`keri-primer.md §3.1`).

**Bespoke / disclosure-specific ACDCs**: a Discloser may issue its *own* ACDC referencing another via an edge, naming the Disclosee as Issuee, to attach context-specific obligations and enable "rich presentation" (combining attributes from multiple edge-referenced ACDCs) "without requiring any new tooling" (§L1860). The SEDI corpus refines this: "'Bespoke' describes an *issuance pattern*... not a credential *type* — so there is no useful generic 'bespoke' schema. What *is* reusable is a **named verification pattern**," and "The club's accepted shape and the holder's issued presentation are one schema viewed from two sides" (`sedi-present-age-portrait/index.md`).

## 11. Real-world credential patterns and what schemas enforce

**vLEI** is the production proof point (ISO 17442-3, finalized Oct 2024): GLEIF -> Qualified vLEI Issuers -> legal entities -> role credentials -> individuals, each hop a SAID-linked, schema-pinned edge. "A verifier receiving an employee's credential can traverse the chain back to GLEIF without consulting a trust registry. The entire graph is self-certifying" (`acdc-vc-diff.md §Real-World Validation`). Concretely, the Legal Entity vLEI carries edge `qvi` -> the QVI credential with `s` const-pinned to the QVI schema, and the ECR Authorization vLEI carries edge `le` -> the LE vLEI with `s` const-pinned to the LE schema SAID (`public-schema` legal-entity/ecr-authorization credentials). The chaining "eliminates the need for trust registries" whose rise "is proof that this conceptual gap needs plugging" (`x509-prob.md`).

**SEDI (Self/State-Endorsed Digital Identity)** applies KERI's root-of-trust to civil identity: "The state endorses an identity; it does not create one" — withdraw the endorsement and the identity persists because the holder, not the state, holds the key (`sedi-id/index.md`). Schema *enforces* minimalism: exactly four attributes (name, birth date, image, Utah residence), with name and residence decomposed into separate nested blocks so "Prove Utah resident" is possible without the street line — because "a single residence block would force over-disclosure on every verifier who only needs jurisdiction." The image is committed by digest with holder-carried bytes; a download URL was rejected as "phone-home by another name" and an availability chokepoint. `sedi-guardian` enforces the anti-impersonation invariant: the guardian holds the credential and the ward is named only by a `subject` edge — "Collapsing the two (guardian-as-subject) is the classic impersonation/commingling failure the whole field warns against" — and it is registry-bound (`ri` mandatory) because guardianship "terminates dynamically... a verifier MUST check current status, not just the signature and dates."

**GCD (Generalized Cooperative Delegation)** is the applied delegation model. The delegation relationship itself is "not embodied in a credential" but in the cooperative two-way AID binding (delegate inception + delegator interaction); GCD's job is to say *what actions are expected and what constrains them*. Its `constraints` container is **fail-closed** (`additionalProperties: false`; an unrecognized key MUST deny), while its descriptive `facet` container is open/forward-compatible — "the open-vs-closed choice tracks descriptive-vs-gating." The `acts` grid crosses five effects (observe/create/modify/preserve/destroy) with six state-kinds (info/record/commitment/authority/resource/relationship): "the two axes of one coordinate, neither meaningful alone." A consequence reviewers must note: "these credentials do not support graduated disclosure. All constraints must be disclosed every time a verifier is evaluating delegated authority" — because within a field values are ORed and across fields ANDed, so partial disclosure would silently loosen the gate (`gcd/index.md`). This is the general rule: **authority/legal credentials are disclosed whole; identity/attribute credentials support graduated disclosure.**

**The Dossier** is itself a valid ACDC used as a curated *bundle of evidence*: "The primary payload of a dossier is not a set of direct claims, but rather a graph of references to external evidence." What the issuer's signature attests is composition/integrity, "NOT veracity" (`dossier-spec-body.md`). This encodes the authenticity-vs-veracity distinction — authenticity is mechanizable, veracity "inherently require[s] subjective assessments of reputation" (`authenticity-vs-veracity.md`). The Dossier adds joint-issuance threshold operators (`MxN`, `RMxN`, `MxQ`, `RMxQ`) in an edge-group's `o`, weighted so Endorsed slots sum to >= unity — "the same fractionally weighted threshold KERI uses for key-event signing thresholds (`kt`)." Its key contrast with group multisig: joint issuance "relies on logic within the ACDC layer," letting members contribute endorsements "at different times and via different channels without immediate impact on a shared KEL," decoupling validity from synchronous key-event coordination. Foreign material (W3C VC, ISO mDL, PDFs, X.509) is bridged via a Foreign Artifact wrapper ACDC, never referenced directly — "Direct reference to non-ACDC material without a wrapper is NOT RECOMMENDED." The FAA corpus makes the trust stance explicit: a bridge issuer's `art_posture` and `rev_latency` fields let "verifiers to treat the FAA as a proxy for the foreign credential, assuming they trust the FAA issuer" — trust in a bridge is "explicit and issuer-scoped, not systemic."

**The negative corpus** (what real schemas *reject* in CI) is the clearest statement of schema teeth: GCD rejects an unknown key inside `constraints` (fail-closed), `terminatingEvents` without a `validUntil` backstop, and two-sided braces in `acts`; `sedi-id` rejects a block missing its blinding nonce, a missing issuee, or an image missing its digest; `sedi-present-age-portrait` rejects a registry-bound (`rd`) presentation and a non-I2I edge operator (`sedi-present-age-portrait/invalid/`). These rejections are where schema stops being documentation and becomes enforcement.

## 12. What ACDC corrects, and what remains uncertain

**Where ACDC explicitly corrects outsider priors** (well-established, spec-grounded): direct-signing of credentials (rejected — forces mass revocation on rotation, L1675); phone-home revocation via OCSP/CRL (rejected via Observer/Registrar split, L1693); dynamic/URL-dereferenced schemas (rejected — schema-revocation and semantic-malleability attacks, L194); treating compact form as private (a public compact ACDC is still rainbow-attackable, L501); bundled multi-claim credentials (refactorable into a graph of separately-disclosable chained ACDCs, "obviating the need for Selective Disclosure," L2739); centralized namespace registries (unnecessary — content-address plus content-addressable schema is the namespace, L2936); over-investing in cryptographic unlinkability ("diminishing returns" without contractually-protected disclosure, L2785); and privacy-as-end-goal (reframed to exploitation-protection, L1659).

**Where the ground truth is softer than the prose** (uncertain / load-bearing assumptions a reviewer must verify against HEAD): the DI2I operator is unimplemented (`NotImplementedError`, `verifying.py:368`); m-ary edge-group operators and DAG acyclicity are read but not enforced (landmines L16/L17); the credential `Verifier` may not re-run `verifyAnchor` on presentation, potentially undermining the anchor-position (retrograde) defense (landmine L15, medium confidence); and disclosure/rule clauses are commitments for application-layer or human enforcement, not predicates the reference verifier evaluates. None of these are protocol defects — they are implementation-maturity gaps on the keripy 2.0-dev line, and all were re-confirmed present as of commit `60ab9e08`. The honest posture is Hardman's own: ACDC's constraints today "are constraints of *youth*, not of *architecture*" (`wtbo §6`). A reviewer who conflates the two — who reads an unimplemented operator as a design flaw, or an unenforced rule as a broken guarantee — is making the category error the whole corpus is built to prevent.

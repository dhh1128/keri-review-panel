# 14 — Identifiers, Trust Bases and Reputation {#identifiers}

## Thesis {#thesis}

KERI secures identifiers, not identities, and the order matters. An AID proves who controls it and nothing else: not which person or organization stands behind it, and not whether what that controller says is true. Sam Smith's identifier theory supplies the missing layer. A trust basis is what an identifier's security rests on, and there are three kinds: autonomic (a self-certifying root of trust), algorithmic (a ledger) and administrative (a trusted entity). A trust domain is the set of statements verifiably issued under one AID, and inside it a human-meaningful name can be made secure by an anchored authorization, which turns it into a legitimized identifier (LID) written as the couplet `aid|lid`. Everything an AID cannot prove about its controller is reputation: which entity it belongs to, whether that entity's statements are reliable, whether it is the same entity as another AID. Reputation is measured, contextual behavior; it needs secure attribution before it means anything; and it is what makes an AID worth trusting, because a bare AID is a cheap pseudonym anyone can mint and abandon. Identity assurance is the most widely used form of it.

For a reviewer this separates the questions. Who controls an AID is answered by its key event log, with no authority anywhere. Which entity it belongs to, and whether to rely on what it says, is answered by reputation, which does involve vouching by assurers, by issuers in a credential chain, and by the relying party's judgment. A critique that asks who vouches for an AID's control has imported a prior KERI removed; a design that assumes control settles identity has skipped a layer KERI's own author says is required.

## Status {#status}

Almost nothing here is normative. The identifier theory, reputation doctrine, trust-basis taxonomy, LIDs, the PAC trilemma and the cheap-pseudonymity argument are [proposal]: Sam's whitepapers, slide decks and one GitHub discussion. None of these constructs appears in the KERI or ACDC specification at the pins in [Sources](#sources), and no keripy code computes or stores reputation. The KERI specification uses "trust basis" twice and applies the authenticity-versus-veracity split once ([section 2](#trust-bases), [section 5](#two-kinds-of-trust)), and the ACDC specification uses "trust domains" twice in passing; "legitimi", "human-meaningful", "trilemma", "pseudonym", "Zooko" and "identity assurance" are absent from both. Where this chapter says KERI holds something, it means KERI's author argues it, unless a [spec] marker says otherwise.

Slide quotes are headline bullets cited by slide title. Some lineage papers are co-authored, as their Sources entries say. Conference captions are used only for framing, and two are from speakers other than Sam; [architect] marks Sam's spoken words. The did:webs specification is marked [proposal] here, which under-claims a document that is a specification in its own standards track outside the KERI, ACDC and CESR lines. The written statements of cheap pseudonymity and of KERI's choice within the PAC trilemma are recent (discussion #1598 and UIT v1.40), so a revision of either could move this chapter.

## 1. Identifier first, identity second {#identifier-first}

Universal Identifier Theory (UIT) opens by choosing its subject: "We use the term identifier system instead of identity system" (UIT > 1.1 Unified Model of Identifiers, p.1 @b47f21597) [proposal]. Identity is defined thinly: "We formally define a (digital) identity to be a (digital) identifier plus attributes." (same) [proposal]. The ordering is a security claim: the model "takes a security-first approach" because "all other properties of an identifier are of little value" unless the identifier is securely controlled (UIT > 1.1 Unified Model of Identifiers, p.2 @b47f21597) [proposal], and UIT's mechanism for control is self-certification (UIT > 1.2 Identifier Control and Trust Basis, p.2-3), the derivation of [chapter 01, section 2](01-foundations-and-worldview.md#autonomic-identity).

Because an identity is an identifier plus attributes, the attributes, including which real entity the identifier belongs to, can be asserted, contested and accumulated in a layer above key state. KERI's core proves control of an identifier ([chapter 01, section 3](01-foundations-and-worldview.md#root-of-trust)); the identity that hangs off it is claims. UIT v1.40 also detaches KERI from any namespace: "A given DID may be a type of AID but not all DIDs are AIDs." and "KERI itself is name space agnostic" (UIT > 1.3 Self-Managing Self-Certifying Identifiers (AIDs), p.6 @b47f21597) [proposal]. Its v1.32 predecessor's sentence that "AIDs may be used as a type of DID." (UIT v1.32 > 1.3 Self-Managing Self-Certifying Identifiers (AIDs), p.6 @416b7ac) is not the current position.

## 2. Three trust bases {#trust-bases}

"A trust basis binds controllers, identifiers, and key-pairs.", and "A trust domain is the ecosystem of interactions (functions) that rely on a trust basis." (SEDI_Tradeoff deck > slide Trust Basis of a Trust Domain @b47f21597) [proposal]. There are three.

- Autonomic: "root-of-trust in verifiable cryptographic proofs of infrastructure with no shared governance" (same slide) [proposal].
- Algorithmic, a ledger: "root-of-trust in verifiable operational infrastructure with shared governance" (same slide) [proposal].
- Administrative, such as DNS plus certificate authorities: "root-of-trust in non-verifiable operational infrastructure with opaque governance" (same slide) [proposal].

Against ledgers, UIT's objection is portability: "Should the ledger go away, so does the root of trust." and "One may characterize a ledger as a centralized logical construct that is governed in a decentralized manner." (UIT > 1.2 Identifier Control and Trust Basis, p.4 @b47f21597) [proposal]. Against administrative bases it is blunter: "Security may be dependent on something as nebulous as employee morale." (UIT > 1.2 Identifier Control and Trust Basis, p.5) [proposal]. [Chapter 07, section 4.1](07-shibboleths-and-anti-patterns.md#global-consensus) and [chapter 07, section 2.5](07-shibboleths-and-anti-patterns.md#administrative-bottom) carry the corresponding tells; the taxonomy lets a reviewer name which basis a design has silently adopted.

The KERI specification uses the term without defining it: KERI "provides the trust basis for a universal AIS" (KERI spec > End-verifiable, L89 @fbdd4a615), and "the identifier’s trust basis is not locked to any given witness or set of witnesses" (KERI spec > Advantages, L1840 @fbdd4a615), the portability argument stated for witnesses. The three-way classification is UIT's.

Two refinements matter in review. A concrete interaction is overlaid per identifier, not per protocol, so each party in an exchange has its own trust basis (SEDI_Tradeoff deck > slide Overlay: Protocol Abstraction vs. Concrete Interaction) [proposal], and the weakest basis a decision depends on bounds it. And cross-domain trust is appraisal: "Maximal trust transitivity happens in an interaction when the trust bases of all parties are mutually appraisable" (SEDI_Tradeoff deck > slide Trust Domain Appraisability @b47f21597) [proposal], which the deck poses as a question, not a result.

## 3. The trust domain and the legitimized identifier {#trust-domain}

"The trust domain of an AID is the set of statements that may be securely verified as being issued by the controller of that AID" (UIT > 1.3 Self-Managing Self-Certifying Identifiers (AIDs), p.6 @b47f21597) [proposal]. Each statement is checked against the keys authoritative when it was issued ([chapter 03, section 11](03-key-management-and-identifier-lifecycle.md#as-of-anchor)); UIT calls this the persistent authorization model, under which a statement "signed with the set of authoritative keys at the time of its signing is valid until revoked" (UIT > 4.1 Authorization Models, p.20) [proposal].

A human-meaningful name alone has "no inherent security property of a human-meaningful identifier. This makes them insecure by default." (UIT > 1.4 Legitimized Human Meaningful Identifiers (LIDs), p.6 @b47f21597) [proposal]. An AID's controller, and only that controller, can legitimize a name within its trust domain by a verifiable authorization; the result is a LID, written `aid|lid`, where the bar is the authorization (UIT > 1.4, p.6-7) [proposal]. The AID supplies the security and the LID the meaning.

UIT offers the couplet as its resolution of Zooko's triangle. Global scarcity disappears because "Scarcity only occurs within the trust domain of each AID but is completely managed by the controller of that trust domain (AID)." (UIT > 1.4, p.7) [proposal]. UIT faults ledger-based resolutions because there "not one identifier but a combination of identifiers is needed to solve the trilemma" (UIT > 1.1 Unified Model of Identifiers, p.2), yet the couplet is itself a combination. The claim therefore amounts to saying that a combination is principled when the joining element is a verifiable authorization in a self-certifying trust domain and unprincipled when it is a registration on someone else's ledger. That is Sam's argument, with no outside assessment in the corpus.

### 3.1 Primary, secondary and tertiary identifiers {#identifier-classes}

AIDs are primary. "Secondary identifiers are LIDs and are not self-certifying but are secured under the aegis of an associated primary identifier's trust domain." Everything else is tertiary: "tertiary identifiers are unsecured and may only be used internally or where security is unimportant" (UIT > 1.4 Legitimized Human Meaningful Identifiers (LIDs), p.7 @b47f21597) [proposal]. A secondary identifier appears with its primary or where the primary is known. The class predates KERI — the 2016 alias "is a human friendly globally unique string of text that may be associated with an identity in addition to its primary cryptonym" and "It is a secondary identifier." (ISE > Aliases, p.4 @b47f21597) [proposal] — but UIT added the security condition: a secondary identifier is secure only when an AID's controller has authorized it.

### 3.2 LIDs in practice {#lids-in-practice}

"GLEIF's Legal Entity Identifier (LEI) may be viewed as a type of LID" (UIT > 1.7 Examples of LIDs, p.10 @b47f21597) [proposal], legitimized by a GLEIF root AID, and GS1 codes are treated alike (UIT > 1.7, p.11). "Usually, the issuance of an LID uses an administrative trust basis." (UIT > 1.7, p.10) [proposal]. The couplet secures the binding of the name to the issuing AID, not the issuer's vetting; what changes is that the vetting's output is anchored, attributable and checkable without a callback ([chapter 06, section 2](06-governance-ecosystems-and-interop.md#arbitration)).

did:webs is the cleanest working instance [proposal]: the controller "MUST authorize the full `did:webs` identifier as a designated alias ACDC anchored to the KERI AID" (did:webs spec > Create, L287 @2d84ef2b0), and the example rule text states the LID's boundary exactly: "It does not assert that the controller of this AID has control over the infrastructure or anything else related to the namespace" (did:webs spec > Full Example, L1565 @2d84ef2b0). [Chapter 06, section 5](06-governance-ecosystems-and-interop.md#did-webs) owns the method.

### 3.3 A LID is not an alias {#lid-not-alias}

[Chapter 07, section 9.4](07-shibboleths-and-anti-patterns.md#aliases) says an alias is a memory aid, not evidence, and only AIDs may drive logic. In UIT's terms that is the rule for tertiary identifiers. A LID is a name made verifiable by an anchored authorization from a known AID. Two errors follow: reading the alias rule as forbidding LIDs, and so rejecting a did:webs host name or verifiable LEI as a petname; and treating a bare alias as a LID, and so keying logic on a name nobody legitimized. The test is whether a verifiable authorization from an identified AID binds the name. If it does, logic may key on the couplet; if not, the name is tertiary. Even a LID inherits only the security of its binding, not the reputation of its name: a LID legitimized by an AID nobody recognizes is a secure name in a domain nobody trusts.

## 4. Establishing a root AID by well-known reputational association {#well-known-association}

Every chain of trust starts at a root AID, which is unique but random, so nothing about it says whose it is. "The process that establishes an AID as the root identifier with an organization may be called a well-known reputational association." Its security condition is social: "Once this connection between root AID and organization becomes common knowledge, then it is secure." (UIT > 1.6 Root AID Establishment via Well Known Reputational Association, p.9 @b47f21597) [proposal]. The mechanism is redundancy across channels the entity controls or that already know it, such as RFC 8615 `/.well-known/` paths or registrations with vetting bodies: "The strength of the well-known establishment is multiplied by the number of such associations, i.e., it provides a reputational source of trust." (UIT > 1.6, p.9) [proposal]. "Consistent common knowledge is the root of reputational trust" (UIT > 1.6, p.10) [proposal]. Once the root is established, every further link is a signed statement under its keys, so the reputational step happens once.

This is the conceptual ancestor of the KERI specification's OOBI-with-MFA pattern ([chapter 06, section 2](06-governance-ecosystems-and-interop.md#oobi-mfa-arbitration)), which bounds itself as UIT does not: the pattern "does not change the trust model of an OOBI", and it "governs only whether the recipient is willing to bootstrap from the OOBIs at all" (KERI spec > OOBI with MFA, L2838 @fbdd4a615) [spec].

### The tension with the OOBI shibboleth {#well-known-tension}

UIT says an association's strength is multiplied by the number of well-knowns; [chapter 07, section 2.6](07-shibboleths-and-anti-patterns.md#multiple-well-knowns) says publishing an AID at several well-knowns does not strengthen the AID binding, and names as the tell "any sentence in which the count of well-knowns appears as an input to an authenticity judgment". Both are right, about different bindings. The binding between an AID and its keys and endpoints is cryptographic, OOBI resolution verifies it, and no number of well-knowns improves it. The binding between an AID and an entity — whether this is the AID GLEIF or the State of Utah actually uses — cannot be established by cryptography at all, because an entity is not a key; it is a reputational fact, and redundancy across independent channels is how reputational facts become credible. So the count of well-knowns is never an input to an authenticity judgment about key state and is a legitimate input to a reputational judgment about which entity an AID belongs to. This chapter's synthesis: a reviewer meeting a root-AID bootstrap should ask which binding each step establishes, and expect the reputational step to be explicit, redundant and done once.

## 5. Two kinds of trust, and the order between them {#two-kinds-of-trust}

UIT's first kind of trust is in the cryptographic root: who said something, and whether they were consistent. "But we may not trust in the veracity of what was said. For this, we need the second type of trust." (UIT > 1.5 Reputational Trust, p.8 @b47f21597) [proposal]. That second kind is reputational: "Reputation is a measure of accumulated consistent behavior." (same). This is the authenticity-versus-veracity split of [chapter 06, section 1](06-governance-ecosystems-and-interop.md#authenticity-veracity), in an earlier written form.

UIT adds an order: "First, authenticity (AID), and then veracity (LID)." (UIT > 1.5, p.8) [proposal]. Reputation needs provably consistent attribution, so "Provable trust in the AID mechanism enables provable trust in the entity behind the LID mechanism." (UIT > 1.5, p.9) [proposal]. Hardman calls the axes orthogonal and UIT makes the second depend on the first, and these are compatible: the judgments are independent, since an authentic statement may be false, while a veracity judgment is useful only if the statement is attributable. Cite Hardman for the analysis and UIT for the dependency. UIT's "veracity (LID)" is shorthand for the reliability of the legitimizing authority, since "we rely on the reputational integrity of the controller to trust that a given LID has been authorized correctly" (UIT > 1.5, p.9) [proposal].

The KERI specification draws the split once, about certificate authorities: a CA certificate "merely provides evidence of the authenticity of the assignment of the mapping but not evidence of the veracity of the mapping" (KERI spec > Overcoming existing security overlay flaws, L81 @fbdd4a615) [spec]. did:webs defines "the term decentralized trust includes verifiability, confidentiality, and privacy, but excludes veracity of the content." (did:webs spec > Introduction, L81-82 @2d84ef2b0) [proposal], and assigns veracity to "(personal) evaluation of available reputational data and verifiable credentials (VCs)" (same, L83-84): what decentralized trust cannot reach, reputation must.

## 6. What reputation is {#reputation-defined}

The definition has held for a decade: reputation is "A considered evaluation (measure) of past behavior used to predict future behavior." and "Confidence improves with contextual similarity." (AR_ACDC_Rep deck > slide Reputation @b47f21597; the 2017 deck has the same lines) [proposal]. The 2025 form is "Reputation is a behavior-based contextual predictor that enables an interaction." (SEDI_Tradeoff deck > slide Reputable Authenticatable Pseudonymity (RAP) @b47f21597) [proposal]. Reputation is about behavior, it predicts, and it is bounded by context; "Contextual predictors are more powerful", "Behavior based predictors are more credible", and "Transitive predictors are more portable" (AR_ACDC_Rep deck > slide What is reputation AI?) [proposal].

### 6.1 Reputes, not ratings {#reputes-not-ratings}

Reputation is computed from recorded behavior, not from scores: "Instances of behavior = reputational events or reputes for short" (AR_ACDC_Rep deck > slide Computational reputation @b47f21597) [proposal], and "Reputation from reputed behavior allows for re-scoping, re-weighting, re-combination, and re-evaluation of collected reputes (not so for ratings)" (same slide). A rating has collapsed evidence into one judgment in one context; a record of behavior can be re-read for a new one. It is the same preference for evidence over verdicts that makes a watcher report duplicity rather than distrust an identifier ([chapter 07, section 8.2](07-shibboleths-and-anti-patterns.md#watcher-enforcement)).

### 6.2 Reputation is not a set of credentials {#not-credentials}

"Reputation is not a set of verifiable claims but a set of verifiable claims can contribute to a reputation" (ReputationIIW2017 deck > slide Issues @b47f21597) [proposal]. A credential is an issuer's attributable assertion; a reputation is a relying party's evaluation, which may count that assertion as one input, weighted by the issuer's reputation and the closeness of contexts. That keeps reputation on the relying party's side and keeps a credential from being read as a verdict.

### 6.3 Why reputation is the motive {#reputation-motive}

On its author's account reputation is the reason the program exists [proposal]: "decentralized identity is essential to decentralized reputation which is essential to decentralized 2 sided networks." (IdentityAndReputation.md, paragraph 2 @b47f21597). The economics follow: "Curation is applied reputation" and "Portable Reputation is a potential meta-platform" (AR_ACDC_Rep deck > slides Curation and Meta-platform) [proposal]. The founding sentence keri.one carries first appears in a 2019 co-authored paper: "a reputation is meaningless without an underlying identity and an identity is valueless without a credible reputation." (CooperationBeatsAggregation.md > Identity and reputation systems as trust brokerages, L132 @b47f21597) [proposal], and such a system "enables trust to be transitive between contexts to some degree" (same, L136).

This explains what looks like a privacy concession. If identity exists so that behavior can accumulate into reputation, persistence across rotation is the point, since an identifier that dies with its first key cannot accumulate anything. Within a context, "the secure attributability of an AID, together with its perfect correlatability, enables secure reputational trust (good or bad) in that AID" (SEDI_Tradeoff deck > slide Relationship Properties @b47f21597) [proposal]. Correlatability inside a context is the feature; across contexts it is the hazard, where the PAC trade of [section 9](#pac-trilemma) begins.

## 7. Reputation by reference and by behavior {#reference-and-behavior}

The 2026 talks named two routes. Phil Windley called the first reputation by reference, "essentially how we bootstrap things" (KERIConf26 caption, Phil Windley, Digital Identity Needs a Legal Foundation, 23:02), as when a diploma chains through a university to its accreditors and the state; the second is reputation by behavior, accumulated from direct interaction. Sam lists "reputation by reference" among UIT's contributions (KERIConf26 caption, Samuel Smith, State of the KERI Suite, 30:30) [architect], though UIT describes the mechanism, a chain of authorizations from a well-known root, without the name. In his older vocabulary, by-behavior is direct reputing and by-reference is intermediated, with a "Reputery = Indirect Source (Implied Reputer) or Intermediate Source" between the behavior and the relying party (ReputationIIW2017 deck > slide Indirect and intermediated reputing @b47f21597) [proposal]. Asked whether reputation by reference is not also accumulated behavior, the answer in Windley's Q&A was "It is!, but it's at a distance." The difference is whom the relying party trusts: "In reputation you explicitly trust the reputer." (KERIConf26 caption, Phil Windley, Digital Identity Needs a Legal Foundation, 27:09 and 28:09; speaker unattributed). By reference, the relying party trusts the reputer's observation; by behavior, only its own.

### 7.1 ACDC chains as the carrier {#acdc-carrier}

The SEDI deck names ACDC chaining as the carrier for reputation by reference: "Verifiable reputation via ACDC chained evidence dossiers" and "Multiple roots-of-trust for reputation" (SEDI_Tradeoff deck > slide Unique features of KERI/ACDC that require Issuer linkability @b47f21597) [proposal]. Each edge is an issuer's attributable statement, and a relying party walks the chain to roots whose reputations it already holds ([chapter 05, section 5](05-acdc-and-verifiable-data.md#edges)), weighing each of several roots by its own reputation, which repeats [section 4](#well-known-association) at every root.

No specification or implementation defines a reputation computation over ACDC chains, a repute schema, or reputation by behavior as credentials. Windley's suggestion that receipts become credentials for scoring markets is a talk. Sam's 2022 deck Automated Reasoning and ACDCs contains the 2016-2018 reputation material and reasoning slides, with no slide showing an ACDC as reputation input (AR_ACDC_Rep deck, read in full, `raw/20` section 2.7); it shows that Sam linked ACDCs to reputation, not a design.

### 7.2 Transitivity across a delegation {#delegation-transitivity}

The one place the theory reaches the protocol is the `DID` trait. Sam says it exists "to formally signal that the controller of the delegated AID is identical to the controller of the delegating AID" (keripy GitHub > discussion #1598, body of 2026-08-09, R-016, https://github.com/WebOfTrust/keripy/discussions/1598) [proposal], so that "any reputation earned by the delegator is also imbued in the delegatee", and misbehavior reflects back, and that it is needed "because not all delegated AIDs have the same legal controlling entity as their delegator AID." (same). [Chapter 03, section 8](03-key-management-and-identifier-lifecycle.md#delegation-trait-rulings) carries the rulings and specification history, and [chapter 07, section 8.4](07-shibboleths-and-anti-patterns.md#did-trait) the tell that reads `DID` as transitive endorsement.

What this chapter adds is the reason. `DID` is a statement about the second binding of [section 4](#well-known-association), AID to entity, made by the controller and countersigned by the delegator's anchoring seal. It transfers reputation because reputation attaches to entities, and it does not transfer authority, which is why it does not propagate to further delegations and why the specification states no validator consequence ([chapter 03, section 8](03-key-management-and-identifier-lifecycle.md#did-effect-unspecified)). Reputation is the relying party's to compute, so the protocol carries the assertion and stops. An entity splits itself across delegated AIDs because "One way to avoid common mode sources of systemic failure is to use multivalent key management infrastructure." (keripy GitHub > discussion #1598) [proposal], which UIT develops for an organization's root AID (UIT > 5 Multi-valent Key Management Infrastructure for an Entity, p.23 @b47f21597) [proposal].

## 8. Cheap pseudonymity and identity assurance {#cheap-pseudonymity}

An AID is self-certifying, which is its strength, and anyone can make one, which is its weakness. AIDs "are just cheap cryptographic pseudonyms (cryptonyms) for the controlling entity that holds the private keys the control the AID." (keripy GitHub > discussion #1598, body of 2026-08-09, https://github.com/WebOfTrust/keripy/discussions/1598; "the control" is the source's typing) [proposal], so "an AID is not very useful for real world interactions between controllers unless a given AID is no longer cheap." (same). Sam said the same at the conference: identity assurance "solves one problem. It's called the cheap pseudonymity problem", because "nobody can trust an AID that anybody can create." (KERIConf26 caption, Samuel Smith, The Digital Identity Tradespace, 36:15 and 36:37) [architect]. The remedy is cost: "And for reputation to be useful and meaningful it must be costly." (keripy GitHub > discussion #1598) [proposal]. Abandoning a costly cryptonym abandons its reputation.

This is why KERI's control guarantee is necessary and not sufficient. Self-certification removes every authority from the question of control ([chapter 01, section 3](01-foundations-and-worldview.md#root-of-trust)) but not the cost of the question of trust, and a design that trusts a freshly incepted AID because its log verifies has confused the two. This chapter's synthesis: the reflex that nobody vouches for the identifier is correct for control and wrong for trust.

### 8.1 Identity assurance as costly reputation {#assurance-as-reputation}

"One type of costly reputation is identity assurance." The controller discloses its legal identity to a prover, and "The prover issues a perpetually verifiable assertion that binds the AID to that real identity. That AID now is not cheap." (keripy GitHub > discussion #1598) [proposal]. Sam ranks it first because "identity assurance is the form of reputation most widely used in our society" (same). Persistence is what makes the cost worth paying: assurance is "amortized over the lifespan of unbounded term (persistence) AIDs" (SEDI_Tradeoff deck > slide Unique features of KERI/ACDC that require Issuer linkability @b47f21597) [proposal], so a rotation-surviving identifier is proofed once. [Chapter 06, section 3](06-governance-ecosystems-and-interop.md#sedi-architecture) owns SEDI's use of it.

### 8.2 Assurance as accumulated weak bindings {#weak-bindings}

The SEDI deck shows several assurers, each asserting a weak binding between a person and an AID's controller: "Relatively weak binding reinforced by multiple bindings = reputation" (SEDI_Tradeoff deck > slide Identity Assurance and Reputation @b47f21597) [proposal]. It is [section 4](#well-known-association) applied to people: no single proofing act is strong, and independence across assurers is what makes the binding credible. The binding of an AID to a person is therefore never cryptographic, however strong the AID; its strength is bounded by the assurers' reputations and independence, and an assurance credential is authentic while its veracity is its assurer's reputation. Out-of-band authentication at relationship setup "also provides protection from cheap pseudonymity" (SEDI_Tradeoff deck > slide OOB Setup) [proposal], and a relationship "sufficiently authenticated and is therefore reputable (not cheap) can be used to issue the equivalent of a letter of introduction" (SEDI_Tradeoff deck > slide Relationship Formation Sub-Protocol) [proposal]: reputation by reference for one relationship. [Chapter 12, section 5](12-confidentiality-and-secure-messaging.md#contexts) owns that protocol.

### 8.3 Reputable pseudonymity {#rap}

Cost need not mean a legal name: "RAP = Trust based on contextual behavior that is securely cryptonymously attributable but without linkage to an actual person or legal entity." (SEDI_Tradeoff deck > slide Reputable Authenticatable Pseudonymity (RAP) @b47f21597) [proposal]. Its limit is on the same slide: "there is no practical pseudonymity without strong counterincentives for 2nd parties to share contextual behavior data." (same). A pseudonym survives only if those who see its behavior are deterred from linking it, which puts the burden on the contractual layer of [chapter 06, section 4](06-governance-ecosystems-and-interop.md#layered-privacy). In the 2026 talks RAP grows into RAP-C3, Sam's one-line summary of KERI (KERIConf26 caption, Samuel Smith, The Digital Identity Tradespace, 03:20) [architect]; the letter the deck expands as "authenticatable" is read as "autonomic" in the talk summaries (`raw/20` section 2.5), and the two should not be normalized.

## 9. The PAC trilemma {#pac-trilemma}

UIT states a trilemma for identifier system security overlays: privacy, authenticity and confidentiality, at most two at the highest degree. "One must make a trade. This is a trilemma for identifier system security overlays." (UIT > 1.8 Identifier System Security Overlay Properties, p.12 @b47f21597) [proposal]. No single cryptographic operation provides all three: non-repudiable signatures give the strongest authenticity and are correlation points, while Diffie-Hellman gives confidentiality without non-repudiation (same). KERI chooses authenticity and confidentiality, "because this best protects all participants from impersonation fraud." (same) [proposal]; v1.32 named only authenticity, and the choice of confidentiality is v1.40's (UIT v1.32 > 1.8 Identifier System Security Overlay Properties, p.11 @416b7ac). The SEDI deck calls it a theorem, "An interaction between two parties may be two of the three, private, authentic, and confidential at the highest degree" (SEDI_Tradeoff deck > slide PAC Theorem — Trilemma @b47f21597) [proposal], but the corpus holds no proof and no outside assessment; it is a design heuristic stated by its author.

The trilemma is the reason KERI's core "does not promise unlinkability, and cannot" ([chapter 06, section 4](06-governance-ecosystems-and-interop.md#layered-privacy)): its bottom layer is non-repudiable signatures. The privacy KERI offers is layered above them ([chapter 06, section 4](06-governance-ecosystems-and-interop.md#decorrelation)), and the stated goal is "not unlinkability, but rather unexploitability through the strongest security." (SEDI_Tradeoff deck > slide KERI's Paradigm Shift: Security First) [proposal]. That is why reputation is compatible with privacy at all: it needs correlation within a context, and the harm is exploitation across contexts. [Chapter 12, section 1](12-confidentiality-and-secure-messaging.md#trade-space) owns the trade space.

## 10. Load-bearing assumptions and conceded weaknesses {#conceded-weaknesses}

### 10.1 Established {#established}

Within the limits of [Status](#status), these are stable across a decade of Sam's writing and consistent with the specifications where they speak: KERI secures control of identifiers, and identity is identifier plus attributes, which are claims; authenticity and veracity are different judgments, a distinction the KERI specification applies to certificate authorities; reputation is a relying party's contextual evaluation of attributable behavior, neither credential nor rating; and a human-meaningful name is secure only as a secondary identifier legitimized in an AID's trust domain, which did:webs implements.

### 10.2 Contested, unbuilt or unverified {#contested}

- No reputation mechanism is specified or implemented; a design relying on a reputation layer relies on theory and on each relying party's own logic.
- Identity assurance is not cryptographic ([section 8.2](#weak-bindings)). A guarantee about an assured AID inherits the assurers' assumptions as well as KERI's ([chapter 02, section 6](02-security-model-and-threat-posture.md#assumptions)).
- Root-AID establishment rests on common knowledge, which no protocol supplies. Before an AID is well known, an attacker who publishes a different AID first or more widely can win the reputational race, and nothing says how a relying party knows the "once" of [section 4](#well-known-association) has arrived.
- The `aid|lid` resolution of Zooko's triangle has no outside assessment, and standards-body LIDs are usually issued on an administrative basis, so the couplet secures their binding, not their vetting ([section 3](#trust-domain)).
- The PAC trilemma is stated without proof, and the confidentiality half of KERI's choice is carried by protocols outside the KERI specification ([section 9](#pac-trilemma)).
- Reputable pseudonymity is conditional on deterrence the protocol cannot supply ([section 8.3](#rap)).
- `DID` carries an entity-equivalence assertion with no specified consequence, and no governance framework read for this corpus consumes it, so reputation's transitivity across delegation is an intent with no consumer ([section 7.2](#delegation-transitivity)).
- Unread sources: the captions of Sam's Tradespace talk stop at 37:02, as he begins a GLEIF analogy for identity assurance; the 2015 Open Reputation papers the decks cite were read for title pages only (`raw/20` section 7.2); and UIT v1.40 drops, unexplained, a v1.32 sentence about a delegator replacing a delegate's identifier prefix (`raw/20` section 1.8), on which this chapter does not rely.

### 10.3 Where KERI corrects the outsider's prior {#outsider-priors}

Asking who vouches for an AID is the right question about the entity behind it and the wrong one about its control; KERI answers the second with a log and leaves the first to reputation ([section 8](#cheap-pseudonymity)). Treating a credential as a reputation collapses an issuer's assertion into a relying party's evaluation ([section 6.2](#not-credentials)). Equating privacy with unlinkability measures KERI against a property its author argues no system can have together with strong authenticity, and against a harm, linkage, that the KERI side replaces with exploitation ([section 9](#pac-trilemma)). A reviewer may contest that argument, but may not measure KERI against a goal it explicitly declines.

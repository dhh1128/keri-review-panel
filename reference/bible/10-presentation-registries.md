# Presentation Registries & Issuee-Side Detectability

**Thesis.** KERI's answer to key compromise is not prevention but *detect and recover*, and for the Issuer that answer is already built: because an ACDC's state must be anchored in a TEL that is anchored in the Issuer's KEL, an Issuer can see fraudulent issuances it did not create, without anyone's cooperation. **The Issuee has no equivalent.** A compromised Issuee's keys can present its credentials, and the real Issuee has no place to look. A presentation registry — Sam's original and better name for it is an *Issuee usage registry* — closes that asymmetry by giving the Issuee its own blindable state registry, requiring that a presentation be anchored in it, and thereby letting the Issuee detect presentations it did not make and rotate to invalidate them.

Two things distinguish this from the rest of the presentation design. First, it is **deliberately optional**, and the argument for optionality is quantitative: Issuer compromise is rampant and high-ROI, Issuee compromise is localized and low-ROI, so Issuee-side detectability is a measure an Issuee elects and pays for rather than a protocol requirement. Second, it does **two unrelated jobs with one mechanism** — impersonation-fraud detection and correlation resistance — and they have different preconditions, so a design that satisfies one may not deliver the other. Confusing them is the most likely error in this area.

Status: **pre-normative, unbuilt, and now intended on the written record.** Three things have moved in different directions since the last pass and must be held apart. The **substrate has hardened**: keripy at `b8f60166b` walks a disclosed ACDC DAG during IPEX, verifies each node against its Issuer's blindable-state registry, and anchors `ax`-marked exchanges directly in the sender's KEL. The **feature is still not built**: no code anywhere reads an `rd` from an attribute section, nothing compares a registry's state to a `grant` SAID, and one of the checks that did land forbids by construction the arrangement a presentation registry requires (§7). The **intent is now citable**: what rested a fortnight ago on an unrecorded call now rests on Sam writing about presentation registries in #1627 on 2026-09-02, and on PR #1674 stating on 2026-09-11 that presentation-registry anchoring "will come in a follow up PR" — a statement by an implementer about what was and was not built, usable for status and intent only and never as evidence about the design (§8). The concept appears in no specification on either line. Read what follows as a design that is going somewhere, and that is further from normative text than the machinery around it now is.

## 1. The asymmetry that motivates it

**Issuer-side detectability is mandatory, and already exists [P].** #1095's argument: the catastrophic failure is Issuer key compromise, because "the worst form of fraud with the most widespread potential of harm would be rampant impersonation fraud… This could annihilate trust in the ecosystem fostered by any assurance derived from the Issuer. The ROI to an attacker for compromising the Issuer keys could be huge, especially if that Issuer is a root-of-trust for Identity assurance for an ecosystem." Anchoring answers it: "an impersonator cannot impersonate the Issuer of an ACDC merely by compromising the signing keys of the Issuer. The impersonator MUST anchor the ACDC in a TEL that is anchored in the Issuer's KEL. This anchor enables the Issuer to detect any fraudulent issuances without requiring the cooperation of any other party, such as the Verifier."

That last clause is the KERI-shaped part: detection with no dependency on a counterparty. Sam attaches a comparative claim — "AFAIK, only KERI/ACDC provides such protection in a decentralized identity system" — which is his assessment, not a surveyed result, and should be repeated as such.

**Issuee-side detectability is optional, and the reasoning is explicit [P].** "If a given Issuee's keys are compromised, the impersonation fraud is localized to that specific Issuee. It does not have rampant potential." Three consequences, all from #1095:

1. **Low susceptibility.** "Because the fraud is localized to a single Issuee, the ROI to the attacker for compromising the Issuee's keys is low." And a cheap mitigation already exists: prophylactic rotation, effective "because the verifier always checks the presentation as being signed by the latest keys of the Issuee." Sam names this as distinctive — the Issuee's ability "to rotate signing keys without reissuing the ACDC is a unique super-power of KERI/ACDC as a decentralized identity system."
2. **Localized harm licenses localized measures.** A Verifier could simply require a rotation as part of the presentation. Sam concedes what that costs doctrinally: "This violates the maxim that security not be dependent on a trusted third party, but for localized harm, the party most likely to be harmed is the Verifier (Validator), which provides incentives for them to protect themselves." **Record this as a real exception rather than smoothing it over** — it is one of the few places in the corpus where the no-trusted-third-party maxim is knowingly relaxed, and an adversarial reviewer will find it whether or not we flag it. It is also not the only relaxation in this design: §8 records a second and distinct one, attaching to the presentation registry itself.
3. **The Issuee can elect its own detectability.** That is the usage registry.

**The design consequence of optionality [P].** "This is an optional protection measure. Many Issuees will feel that their key management is sufficient to protect against key compromise and therefore would not want the extra protection and friction of a usage Registrar." A presentation registry means the Issuee runs infrastructure — "the Issuee must have its own Registrar to manage its usage registries." That cost is why the mechanism is elective, and why any argument that it should be mandatory has to answer it.

## 2. The mechanism

**The rule, in its original form [P].** "The ACDC must include a requirement that the ACDC is only verifiable when the presentation of the ACDC by the Issuee is anchored as the latest state in an Issuee-controlled *usage registry*. The verifier won't accept the credential if it's also not anchored in the Issuee's (in addition to the Issuer's) registry" (#1095).

**Developed into IPEX terms [P].** In #1613 the anchored object is the `grant`: "the value of the registry `rd` field in the attribute `a` section MUST be the SAID of the `rip` event of the associated presentation Registry. The Issuee of the associated ACDC, who is also a Grantor of that IPEX, MUST control this registry. The value of the ACDC SAID field in the blinded attribute block of the latest non-vacuous event in that presentation Registry must be the SAID of the `grant` message." Note the field reuse: the slot that normally carries an ACDC SAID carries a `grant` SAID.

**One registry can serve many credentials without leaking which [P].** "A *usage registry* can be employed to track the usage of one or more ACDCs by the Issuee… This means that the registry does not leak specific usage of a given ACDC to third parties" (#1095). Blindable state is what makes many-to-one safe; without blinding, a shared registry would be a correlation engine rather than a defence.

**Detection converted to prevention, at the price of latency [P].** #1095 offers a verifier-side trick that appears nowhere else in the corpus: "by adding a time delay from presentation anchor to acceptance, the verifier can assume that a compromised Issuee would do a recovery, invalidating the fraudulent presentation." Detect-and-recover normally means the damage lands and is then undone; a deliberate acceptance delay gives the real Issuee a window to rotate *before* the presentation is honored. **This is worth more attention than it has had.** It is the only proposal in the corpus that converts Issuee-side detectability into Issuee-side prevention, and its cost — every honest presentation waits — is exactly the kind of tradeoff an EGF should be setting.

**Incentive alignment, stated as a rule of construction [P].** "The verifier has a vested interest in not accepting unanchored presentations as the presentation of an Issuee unanchored ACDC that declares it must be Issuee usage anchored, must be considered fraudulent" (#1095). #1613 sharpens it into a liability claim: a verifier accepting an unanchored grant "is presumptively in violation of any contractual or regulatory protection afforded to the real Grantor by colluding with a fraudulent grantor."

## 3. Two jobs, two different preconditions

This is the section to get right, because the mechanism is the same in both cases and the guarantees are not.

**Job one: impersonation-fraud detection — requires that the Issuer is not the Grantor [P].** The protection works because the requirement is baked in by someone the attacker has not compromised: "an imposter that merely compromises the Grantor's signing infrastructure can't avoid the requirement without also compromising the Issuer." The Grantee enforces it by refusing an unanchored grant; the real Issuee then sees registry events it did not create and rotates.

The hole is self-issuance, and #1613 states it plainly: "For ACDCs that the Grantor self-issues, this is only a vulnerability if the Issuance authentication factor is merely an unanchored signature. An imposter who controls the Grantor's signing infrastructure can create ACDCs whose authentication factor is an unanchored signature." An attacker holding your keys can simply issue itself a credential that demands nothing. **So the fraud-detection property is a property of the three-party arrangement, not of the registry.**

**Job two: correlation resistance — works even self-issued [P].** The problem: if a Grantor anchors a `grant` SAID directly in its public KEL and the Grantee anchors the same SAID in its own, "a third party… would be able to detect the same grant SAID in both the Grantor's KEL and the Grantee's KEL, thereby correlating the IPEX." Anchoring in a blinded presentation registry instead means "neither the grant SAID nor any other artifacts of the granted DAG appear in the KEL of the Grantor," so "a Grantee could anchor every message in the IPEX without providing a point of correlation between the KELs of the Grantor and Grantee."

For this job a Grantor needs no cooperating Issuer: it self-issues a bespoke origin ACDC carrying `i` and `rd` in its attribute section — "the Grantor is both Issuer and Issuee of that bespoke ACDC." One constraint rides along, and it is easy to violate: "the bespoke ACDC itself must use a blinded state Registry for its Issuer authentication so that the SAID of its ACDC is not correlatable. If it uses a direct KEL anchor, then that anchor itself would be correlatable. If it used a bare attached signature… then the ACDC itself would not be perpetually verifiable despite using a perpetually verifiable presentation registry."

**The object that construction reaches for is now specified, though not for this purpose [N1.1].** The ACDC specification's `v1.1` branch at `2362e48a1` makes both halves normative in its own line: "That DAG MUST have exactly one source node, called the origin node," and, where a single exchange conveys several unchained ACDCs, "a DAG MUST be formed by issuing a bespoke ACDC whose node is the origin." That earns the chapter two things it did not have. The single-origin rule is the premise underneath the claim below that one anchor can cover a whole DAG — commit to the origin and you have committed to what is reachable from it — and until this pass the corpus held that premise only on #1613's own say-so. And the bespoke origin #1613 asks a Grantor to self-issue is a specified object rather than an ad-hoc one. What `v1.1` does **not** say is that such an origin needs a blinded state registry, which is exactly the constraint quoted above and exactly what #1627's competing recipe drops; see open question 8.

**Correlation resistance is itself conditional [P].** The registry is "bound to the AID of the Issuee, so it is linkable. A bulk-issued ACDC with independent AIDs can remove this linkage" (#1095). So full unlinkability needs blinded state *and* independent-registry bulk issuance. A presentation registry used naively hides *which* credential was presented while advertising *that* the Issuee presented something.

**Multi-ACDC DAGs [P].** "When multiple ACDCs in the granted DAG each have a different presentation registry ID field value… the verifier must verify all of the anchors." And a precise statement of what a single anchor buys, worth quoting whenever someone over-claims: because the `grant`'s `o` field names the DAG origin, anchoring in one registry "provides a perpetually verifiable proof of the presentation of all ACDCs in that DAG, **but not necessarily a fresh proof of control for all Issuee AIDs in that DAG**. Multiple anchors in different registries provide multiple vectors of detectability and multiple fresh proofs-of-control; the perpetual verifiability is redundant."

One label caution, because it will mislead a careful reader rather than a careless one. The `o` in that sentence is a field of the IPEX exchange message, and the ACDC specification does not define it on either branch; there `o` is the Operator field of an Edge or Edge-group. The two never share a field map, so nothing is actually ambiguous, but the *concept* the `grant`'s `o` names — a DAG with a single origin — is `[N1.1]`, while the field carrying it is `[P]` plus `[K]` and is standardized nowhere.

## 4. The trigger, and the ambiguity blocking it

**The signal is a three-part test [P].** From #1613: the requirement fires when a non-empty `rd` **and** a valid Issuee `i` are at the top level of the ACDC's attribute `a` section, **and** the ACDC's own top-level `rd` is non-empty. Miss any one and the meaning changes: with no Issuee in `a`, an `rd` in `a` imposes no anchoring requirement at all; with the top-level `rd` empty or missing, the `rd` in `a` "refers to an ACDC state registry, not an IPEX presentation registry." **The top-level `rd` is doing disambiguation work**, which is the easiest part of this design to miss when reading #1613 quickly.

**Why disambiguation is needed [P].** The v1 spec also permits `rd` in `a` as a *hidden ACDC state registry*, so one field slot carries two unrelated meanings. #1613 offers EGF-declared purpose with "The default, if not otherwise specified, should be for anchoring presentation exchanges," floats forbidding the hiding case in v1.1, and then argues that case down: its only benefit arises "when the ACDC in compact form is made public, independent of any given presentation, and the fact of it having a registry must remain undisclosed. It is not clear that this is a worthy use case… Given the potentially dubious value, we might want to forbid using the `rd` field in the attribute section for an ACDC state registry."

The section ends unresolved — "Not sure if either is a worthy use case" — and **that unresolved state is what blocks normative text.** You cannot specify a trigger condition on a field whose meaning is still contested.

**What the choice costs, which is more than a labelling nicety [P].** The two readings are mutually exclusive per ACDC, and #1613 says so flatly: "a presentation registry may not be used with an ACDC that uses the `rd` field in the attribute `a` section as an Issuer-controlled ACDC state." One slot, one meaning, fixed at issuance and not recoverable afterwards. So an Issuer choosing the hiding case forecloses Issuee-side detectability on that credential permanently. #1613 uses the same fact to talk itself out of the hiding case — hiding "is not useful with full independent Registry bulk issuance since each presentation could use a different member of the bulk issued set" — so the correlation problem the hiding case solves is already solved better elsewhere, while the price of keeping it, forfeiting presentation registries for that ACDC, is paid nowhere else.

**And `v1.1` did not settle it.** At `2362e48a1` the ACDC specification's §Registry SAID Field and its reserved-fields row are byte-identical to `main` at `f0bd097de`, typo included. None of the three resolutions #1613 floats — EGF-declared purpose, restriction to presentation registries, forbidding the state-registry reading — has been taken up in the branch most likely to carry it. That is a negative result at a named commit rather than a silence, and it means the contested-field material in this section and in §5 stands unchanged and is now dated.

**Field-placement consequence nobody else raises [P].** From #1618: "An exchange anchored in a presentation registry would also need an `rd` on the exchange." If true, this design implies a field addition to `exn`, not only to the ACDC — and that is not accounted for in #1613's `ax` treatment.

## 5. What is normative: one word

**[N]** In `trustoverip/kswg-acdc-specification` `spec/spec-body.md` on `main` @`f0bd097de` (2026-08-28), "presentation registry" does not appear at all and "usage registry" appears exactly once, in a gloss. What does exist:

- The nested-`rd` field's purpose list includes usage. Top-level: "Issuance and/or revocation, transfer, or retraction registry for ACDC" (`:23`). Not-at-top-level: "Issuance and/or revocation, transfer, retraction, **or usage** registry for ACDC when not at top-level" (`:48`). **That word "usage" is the only trace of this design in any specification.**
- Nested `rd` is open-ended, but the examples point elsewhere: it "MAY be used for some other registry, such as an application-specific or **Issuer-specific** registry" (`:85`). The spec does not contemplate an Issuee-controlled registry.
- The spec's rationale for nesting is bulk issuance and graduated disclosure — it "may better facilitate contractually protected disclosure of the bulk-issued registry" (`:326`) — not fraud detection.
- Blindable state registries and `rip` are fully normative, with a worked lifecycle. A `rip` is "a vacuous placeholder that reveals nothing about what will later be issued," which is where #1613's "latest non-vacuous event" phrasing comes from.

**The worked lifecycle also states this chapter's correlation boundary, and states it better than the corpus had [N].** §Registry-Dependent Issuance Lifecycle walks a registry from `rip` through issuance, blinded state, verification and revocation, and closes with two sentences that belong wherever anyone claims a blindable registry buys privacy. The first draws the line between the log and its contents: "The registry *events* remain public and chained (the `rd` and prior links are in the clear); it is the state *content* each event commits to that is protected." The second is the doctrine: **"This is confidentiality of registry state, not anonymity of the credential — issuer, registry, and issuance chronology remain linkable, which is the intended, auditable trade-off."** This is the normative version of what §3 records from #1095 as a caveat — a usage registry is "bound to the AID of the Issuee, so it is linkable" — and it settles the register in which the whole correlation-resistance argument should be made. Linkability of the registry is not a defect the design failed to notice; it is the trade the design chose. Whoever writes spec text for presentation registries will have to say the same thing about an Issuee's registry that this section says about an Issuer's.

**But the lifecycle is Issuer-controlled end to end, and that is a negative result, not an omission [N].** The whole worked example runs Issuer-side: the Issuer incepts the registry, issues, and appends every blindable update, while the Issuee's only role is holding the shared salt. Nothing in it, and nothing elsewhere on either branch, contemplates a registry incepted by the Issuee.

**The negatives, at both pins, dated 2026-09-15.** "Presentation registry" has **zero occurrences** in `spec-body.md` at `main` @`f0bd097de` and at `v1.1` @`2362e48a1`. "Usage registry" occurs **exactly once** at either pin — the same reserved-fields gloss quoted above, which remains the only trace of this design in any specification, on either line. And there is **no Issuee-controlled registry anywhere in `v1.1`**: "Issuee-controlled", "Issuee controlled" and "Issuee's registry" occur zero times at `2362e48a1`, so the statement above — that the spec does not contemplate an Issuee-controlled registry — now holds on the forward line as well as on the standardizing one.

**One wording tension to fix before any spec text [N/P].** "Because the Issuer `i` field appears in the `rip` event, the Registry SAID, `rd` field value cryptographically binds the Registry to the Issuer AID" (`:2019`). For a presentation registry the incepting AID is the **Issuee**. The spec sentence is about the registry's own incepting controller and is not wrong, but its vocabulary assumes the two coincide, and they do not here. As of 2026-09-15 this has stopped being only a wording problem: keripy at `b8f60166b` now enforces precisely that reading in code, refusing any ACDC whose `i` differs from the registry incepter's (§7). The ambiguity has been resolved in an implementation, in the direction that excludes this design, before it was resolved in a specification.

## 6. Where this sits against Registrar/Observer and KRAM

**It extends the governance split to a third party.** `bible/05-acdc-and-verifiable-data.md` §Registrar/Observer records Sam's framing that the split deliberately replays witness/watcher one layer up: "we have witnesses that are controlled by controllers, watchers that are controlled by verifiers… We have registrars controlled by the issuers, and observers controlled by the verifiers" (KERIcon 2026, `raw/14`). A presentation registry adds an Issuee-controlled Registrar to that picture — the Holder, previously the one party in the triangle running no state infrastructure, now runs some. **This is the synthesis the corpus does not state anywhere**, and it makes the design look less like a bolt-on: "We shouldn't have shared governance" applied consistently gives every party to a transaction its own state service.

Sam's KERIcon phrasing is the clean version: presentation registries let "presenters detect compromise of their proofs that are in their credentials that they're presenting. So they themselves will protect it from fraud, not just the issuer." (Edited auto-caption — read `raw/14` §0 before quoting it to anyone.)

**It sits inside KRAM's window [P].** "in order to pass KRAM, a `grant` message anchor must happen in a timely fashion relative to the IPEX exchange. The `grant` message… must be created, then anchored, and then pass the receiver's KRAM" (#1613). A presentation registry therefore inserts a registry write into the critical path of a freshness window — and #1095's verifier-side delay deliberately adds more latency on top. Nothing in the corpus connects the two numerically. See `bible/09-kram-and-request-authentication.md` §3 on why full KRAM's windows can be long enough to absorb this, and §9 on the absence of any window-class policy guidance.

**And the registry anchor does not stand in for the ordinary one [P].** #1613's 2026-09-02 edit makes the layering explicit: "KRAM does not recognize or process a presentation registry-based authentication factor for the grant message," so "a presentation registry-anchored grant message is always double-authenticated," and the duplication is "unavoidable because the processing of presentation registry validation happens higher up in the stack than grant message authentication." Two consequences. The registry write is **added** to the signature or direct KEL anchor rather than replacing either, which makes the latency question in open question 5 worse than it looked, not better. And the absence of any presentation-registry handling in keripy's `kraming.py` is conformance rather than a gap — §7's negative is about `ipexing.py`, where such a check would belong. A verifier that treats registry anchoring as *replacing* the signature check will reject valid grants or accept invalid ones, depending on which way it errs.

**It is independent of, and stronger than, `ax` [P].** "Notwithstanding the presence or absence of the `ax` field, when an ACDC in a granted DAG meets the requirements for a Issuer signaled presentation anchor registry… then that `grant` message MUST be anchored in that registry for the `grant` to be valid. Otherwise, the presumption is that the grant is fraudulent." `ax` lets a party *request* perpetual verifiability; a presentation registry lets an Issuer *impose* it at issuance time, and the Grantor cannot opt out later. See `bible/08-presentation-architectures-and-ipex.md` §9. The two have since come apart in implementation as well as in design — `ax` is built, the presentation registry is not — which §7 states as its own finding.

**Scope judgment on which registry may be used [P].** "There is no security advantage to using a presentation registry specified by the Grantor, as the main purpose… is to allow the Grantor to detect a compromise of its signing key infrastructure… Thus, the main use case for impersonation fraud detection of the Grantor requires a pre-specified presentation registry **by an Issuer different from the Grantor**." On tooling, #1613 declines to build anything special: "we should use the existing tooling for a bulk-issued ACDC to handle it."

## 7. keripy: the substrate hardened, the feature did not arrive

Re-read at `b8f60166b` (2026-09-15), superseding this section's `4df8e4a8` (2026-09-01) statement. A great deal landed in those two weeks — `ipexing.py` went from 722 to 1,622 lines, and PRs #1643, #1644 and #1674 added IPEX DAG nesting, an origin walk, durable stores and `ax` anchoring — so the interesting result is how little of it is this feature, and that one of the three negatives below has changed sign.

**[K] Present.** `src/keri/acdc/regeventing.py` is the v2 registry event layer — `_validateRip`, `_validateUpdate` for blindable updates, `vetAnchor` checking a TEL event against its issuer's KEL, and `vet()` returning verified registry state. `src/keri/acdc/registraring.py` provides `Regery` ("Local manager for V2 ACDC blindable state registries"), `Registry` and `Registrar` ("facade for V2 registry inception and blindable state updates"). `src/keri/acdc/messaging.py` builds ACDCs with a `regid` and an `iseaid`.

**[K] New at this pin: IPEX now verifies registry-backed ACDCs.** `IpexHandler` takes a `Regery` and walks the disclosed DAG, calling `_verifyIssuerAuthNode` on each node, which delegates to `regeventing.vet`. **This is Issuer authentication, not presentation anchoring**, and the docstring names which `rd` it reads: "When the ACDC body has a top-level `rd` field, that field names the registry whose TEL history must authenticate the node." So the honest way to describe the gap has changed. It is no longer that registries are unwired into presentation; it is specifically that the *Issuee's* registry is unwired.

**[K] New at this pin: direct KEL anchoring of `ax`.** When `ax` is truthy on an `agree`, `grant` or `admit`, `IpexHandler.verify` requires a sender source-seal couple resolving to an event in the **sender's own** KEL that seals the exact message SAID. It also enforces the negotiation itself: a binding reply must preserve the negotiated state exactly, and an `offer` may introduce anchoring but may not drop it.

**One boundary on both of those claims, which bounds how far `[K]` reaches here.** `acdc/ipexing.py` is the V2 IPEX handler, and at `b8f60166b` its `loadHandlers` has **no caller anywhere in `src/keri`**: keripy's own CLI still wires up the V1 handler in `vc/protocoling.py`. So these are claims about code that exists and is tested in the reference implementation, not about behavior any shipped keripy command performs today. Do not read them as stronger than that — and note that the same boundary makes the absence below slightly *less* damning than it reads, since the module where a presentation-registry check would belong is itself not yet in the shipped path.

**[K] Absent — the three negatives, re-checked.** Two hold. One has changed, and not in the direction the design needs.

**One: no code recognizes the three-part signal.** Still true, and now verifiable exhaustively rather than by inspection. Every read of an `rd` key anywhere in `src/keri` is of the **top level** — six sites, across `ipexing.py`, `regeventing.py`, `messaging.py` and the `regid` property in `serdering.py`. **There is no read of `rd` from an `a` section anywhere.** The Issuee `i` in `a` *is* now read, through the new `iseaid` property, but only for edge-operator semantics, in the I2I and DI2I far-node checks. **So keripy now understands both halves of the signal, separately, and combines them nowhere** — which is a more interesting negative than last month's, because the missing piece is no longer a capability but a join.

**Two: no grant-anchor enforcement in a presentation registry.** Still true, and the claim needs restating now that `ipexing.py` is no longer registry-blind. What the new path binds is the **ACDC's** SAID to the registry's head state: `vetBindings` refuses when the registry state's transaction SAID differs from the presented ACDC's own SAID. Nothing anywhere compares a registry's disclosed state to a **`grant` message SAID**, which is what #1613 requires. The `ax` work anchors the `grant` in the *sender's KEL*, which is a different mechanism reaching a different party's log.

**Three: ~~nothing distinguishes an Issuee-controlled registry from an Issuer-controlled one~~ — superseded 2026-09-15.** As written at `4df8e4a8`, this said that a `Registrar` was a `Registrar` and no code consumed the difference; the negative was one of indifference. **At `b8f60166b` something does consume it, and consumes it the wrong way.** `vetBindings` gained an `issuer` parameter and now requires, in its own words, that "the ACDC's own issuer must match the issuer that incepted the presented registry," raising a misbinding error with "Presented ACDC issuer … does not match presented registry issuer" otherwise. **A presentation registry is incepted by the Issuee, so an ACDC vetted against one fails this check by construction.** The check is correct for the Issuer state registry it was written for — v2 ACDCs are not directly signed, so without it a verifier "passes every other obligation while accepting a substituted credential" — and the presumable fix, when presentation registries are built, is to scope it to the top-level `rd` path rather than remove it. But the shape of the negative has hardened: **keripy has moved from not supporting presentation registries to enforcing a rule they break.**

**The three-factor framing's legs have come apart, and this is the load-bearing distinction of the whole section.** #1618 states symmetrically that three authentication factors apply to the presentation and not only to issuance: "Grant is signed (normal)", "Grant is anchored in a presentation registry (issuee-controlled; `rd` on the origin ACDC can force the check)", and "Grant is anchored in a KEL without a presentation registry (a case V1 did not really specify)". Two provenance notes before anyone leans on it. The list is **unnumbered in the source** — the corpus has been referring to these as legs one, two and three, and that numbering is the corpus's, not Ryan's. And #1618's author withdrew it the same day as a duplicate of #1613, so it is not independent corroboration; its value is that it is the compact symmetric statement of a structure #1613 takes a section to give, with a precedence attached: "when multiple authentication factors are either required or provided for the same purpose, the highest-preference factor is used, and the others are ignored."

**At `b8f60166b` the KEL-anchor leg is `[K]` and the presentation-registry leg is not.** keripy implements direct sender-KEL anchoring of a `grant` under a truthy `ax`, and implements nothing for anchoring in a presentation registry, which remains `[P]`. **Nothing about "anchoring landed" may be allowed to blur across both**, because what landed is precisely the leg that needs no registry, no Issuee cooperation and no new infrastructure. PR #1674, which added it and merged on 2026-09-11, says so of itself: "This PR does not implement presentation-registry anchoring or observer-based TEL retrieval. It will come in a follow up PR." That is a written, attributable, dated statement by an implementer about what was and was not built, and this chapter uses it for **status and intent only** — it supports nothing whatever about how a presentation registry would work. That the cheaper leg shipped first is what one would predict from cost, and is evidence about neither design's merits.

**So an Issuee could stand up a usage registry today with shipped tooling, and no verifier would look at it.** Re-checked and unchanged. The gap is still entirely on the verification side, and it is now a gap with a described future attached to it rather than an open one.

**[K] No implementation work is proposed.** Re-searched 2026-09-15 across keripy issues **and** pull requests together, for "presentation registry", "presentation registries", "issuee usage registry", "usage registry" and "issuee registry". There are **no issues at all**, and exactly two pull requests, **neither of which implements one**. The first is our own merged `#1505`, whose mentions record a deliberate decision *not* to use a presentation registry in a bespoke one-time presentation, with a follow-up example flagged and still unwritten; it corroborates nothing. The second is `#1674`, quoted above. The strings "presentation registry", "usage registry" and "issuee registry" appear nowhere in `src/keri` at this pin.

**The narrower statement survives, and is still informative.** #1627's **body**, through its last edit of 2026-09-11, contains zero occurrences of "presentation registry", "usage registry" or "issuee registry". Every mention in that discussion is in the comment stream. So Sam's own survey of how to shape an ACDC presentation does not reach for a presentation registry when laying out the tradespace, and names one only when asked a direct question about anchoring. It remains a class of source this corpus otherwise does not use and the weakest in form — no recording, no transcript, not even approximate wording, unlike `raw/14`'s edited captions — usable for **status and intent only**, never as a quotation and never as evidence about a design detail. What it was load-bearing for on 2026-09-01, two written sources now carry independently. Worth noticing in that direction: the corpus held a true claim on evidence it could not cite, until written sources caught up with it — which is the argument for recording a weak-provenance class explicitly rather than either using it silently or refusing it.

**The novelty claim is unverified.** "nobody else does as far as I've never seen anybody do it" (KERIcon) and "AFAIK, only KERI/ACDC provides such protection" (#1095) are both explicitly hedged by their author. Holder-side presentation logging is not an unheard-of idea in the wider VC world; what is distinctive here is that the log is cryptographically *required* by the credential and enforced by the verifier, rather than being a courtesy audit trail. That narrower claim is defensible; the broad one has not been checked.

**The trusted-third-party concession is real, and there are two of them, not one.** The first is in §1, in Sam's own words: Verifier-enforced protection against Issuee impersonation "violates the maxim that security not be dependent on a trusted third party," justified by localized harm and by the Verifier's own incentive to protect itself. The second is distinct, and it lands on the mechanism this chapter is about. #1613 describes what a presentation registry gives the Grantor as "an Issuer-Verifier-protected mechanism to detect compromise of its signing keys" — protected at both ends by other parties. It needs an Issuer that is a different party and does not collude, because that is the only reason an imposter holding the Grantor's keys cannot simply issue itself a credential demanding nothing (§3). And it needs a Verifier that enforces: "The verifier then enforces the protection by not accepting presentations (grant messages) that are not so anchored." **So the mechanism offered to an Issuee as its own self-protection is two-party-dependent at both ends.** Neither concession is an oversight — both are stated plainly by their author, which is why they belong here rather than in a critic's mouth — but a reviewer who quotes the no-trusted-third-party maxim against this design is right about the tension, and the corpus's residual on that maxim should name the second relaxation as well as the first.

**Fraud protection has a self-issuance hole that its own author documents.** §3. An attacker with the Grantor's keys can self-issue a credential that requires nothing. The mitigation is that high-stakes credentials come from third-party Issuers — which is true and is also an admission that the property lives in the deployment pattern, not the mechanism.

## 9. Open questions

1. ~~**Is this live?**~~ **Answered: yes, and as of 2026-09-15 answerable from written sources.** ~~The answer rested on a first-hand report of a call on 2026-09-01, and on the observation that #1627 does not mention presentation registries at all.~~ Both halves of that basis are superseded (§8): Sam wrote about presentation registries in a #1627 comment on 2026-09-02, and PR #1674 stated on 2026-09-11 that presentation-registry anchoring "will come in a follow up PR". The eleven-month silence on #1095 is real and still does not reflect the design's status. What remains open is everything below, none of which those sources address.
2. **What is it called?** "Issuee usage registry" (#1095), "presentation registry" (#1613, #1618), "user presentation registry" (KERIcon). #1550's *Guardianship for SEDI* uses the same primitive for agent capability control, with a diagram legend reading `rd = issuee usage registry SAID` — so the general thing is not about presentation. **The case for the usage name got stronger this pass:** #1627 proposes a fourth job, a "transfer registry where a state change is not to revoke an ACDC but to provide a forward reference" to a replacement, and the ACDC spec's own `rd` gloss already lists four purposes. Four uses, one of which is presentation, and the name of the whole is the name of that one. The general name should probably be the usage one, with "presentation registry" reserved for the IPEX-anchoring application.
3. **Does `rd`-in-`a` get disambiguated, and how?** EGF-declared purpose, restriction to presentation registries in `v1.1`, or left ambiguous. Unresolved in #1613 and blocking any normative text. **Still open, and now with a dated negative behind it:** at `v1.1` @`2362e48a1` the relevant spec text is byte-identical to `main` @`f0bd097de`, so none of the three resolutions has been taken up in the branch most likely to have carried one (§4). What §4 also newly records raises the stakes — the two readings are mutually exclusive per ACDC and the choice is permanent, so this is not a labelling question. Tracked as open question 7 in `bible/08-presentation-architectures-and-ipex.md`.
4. **Does the exchange need its own `rd`?** #1618 says an anchored exchange "would also need an rd on the exchange," which implies a field addition to `exn` that #1613 does not account for.
5. **What is the latency budget?** Registry write inside KRAM's window, plus a deliberate verifier delay. No number anywhere — and worse than it looked, since §6 records that the registry anchor is *additional* to the signature or KEL anchor rather than replacing either.
6. **What would `rip` spec text say?** The current wording binds a registry to "the Issuer AID" (`spec-body.md` §Registry SAID Field); for a presentation registry the incepting AID is the Issuee. **No longer only a wording question:** keripy at `b8f60166b` enforces that reading and refuses the arrangement outright (§7), so a spec fix now implies a code fix, and the two have to be scoped together.
7. **Who verifies, and against what?** The design says the Grantee must check the anchor. Against the Issuee's Registrar directly, or via an Observer? `bible/05` records that Observers are verifier-controlled and watch registrar state — which is exactly the shape this needs — but no source connects them. PR #1674 pairs the two absences without connecting them either, naming "presentation-registry anchoring or observer-based TEL retrieval" as the follow-up work.
8. **New: two incompatible bespoke origin ACDCs share a name.** #1613 requires the self-issued bespoke origin to use a blinded state registry for its Issuer authentication, or the correlation resistance a presentation registry buys is handed back at the origin node (§3). #1627's SEDI "recipe" pattern specifies the opposite — no registry, no anchor, bare attached signatures, because it is one-time use. Both are Sam, three weeks apart, and both are coherent for their own purpose; they are two different bespoke ACDCs for two different jobs. But neither document says the other exists, and the shared name invites the wrong one. **Any worked example that combines a presentation registry with a #1627-style recipe origin is wrong in a way neither source warns about** — it yields a correlatable presentation and a `grant` whose perpetual verifiability the origin ACDC cannot carry.

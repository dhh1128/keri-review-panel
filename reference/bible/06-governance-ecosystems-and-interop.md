# Governance, Ecosystems & Interop

**Thesis.** KERI draws a hard line between two kinds of trust decision, and everything in this chapter follows from where that line is drawn. On one side is *cryptographic verification* — signatures, SAIDs, hash-chain consistency, anchor-position validity — which is universal, mechanical, observer-independent, and answerable by anyone, anywhere, at any time, with no authority in the loop. On the other side is *ecosystem-defined behavior* — what a credential means, which edge operator makes a chain valid, whether an issuer is fit to issue, what a recipient may do with disclosed data — which is inescapably semantic, contextual, and human- or policy-governed. An Ecosystem Governance Framework (EGF) lives entirely on the second side; it never adjudicates the first. This is the structural fact that lets KERI claim decentralization honestly: because Layer-1 verification depends on no governance body, no EGF, no registry, and no browser-vendor root program, the only thing an ecosystem's governance can do is publish rules that opt-in participants cryptographically bind themselves to — it cannot become a new root of trust or a new choke point. The corollaries occupy the rest of the chapter: who (if anyone) arbitrates ecosystem behavior; how SEDI applies the "endorse-not-issue" inversion to civil identity; why correlation-resistance and a duty of data loyalty are *layered* properties that KERI-core does not and cannot promise (they live in the ACDC/disclosure/governance strata); how semantics cross trust domains without a global namespace authority; what versioning and migration actually cost; how four independent implementations stay byte-compatible; and how KERI scores against SD-JWT/OIDC4VC on a decentralization scorecard — with the honest caveats about where KERI's advantage is architecture and where it is merely youth.

Adversarial reviewers should carry one framing throughout: a critique that faults KERI for "not preventing correlation" or "still needing governance" is usually attacking the wrong layer. KERI-core promises key-state continuity and duplicity-evidence, not unlinkability and not semantic agreement. The interesting questions are whether the *layering* is clean, whether the governance seam is defensible, and whether the migration story survives contact with four codebases.

## 1. What an EGF is, and the two-layer verification split

The load-bearing distinction is stated most cleanly in the Dossier specification's verification doctrine: verification is a **two-layer process**. Layer 1 is cryptographic validation — "confirming signatures, SAIDs, and KEL consistency" — which is "universal, defined by this spec." Layer 2 is semantic validation, which is "necessarily application-specific and requires context-dependent business logic" (Dossier spec §"Layered verification doctrine"). The spec's own illustration is exact: a generic verifier "can verify that an edge labeled 'lunarPropertyDeed' is cryptographically linked, but it cannot know what that means or how to process it." This separation "is what makes the dossier a universal building block for evidence aggregation." An EGF is the artifact that supplies Layer-2 rules for a given ecosystem: what schemas are blessed, what an issuer must do to be trusted, what validity means for a particular chain of authority, what obligations attach to disclosed data.

That the Layer-2 rules are genuinely *outside* the cryptographic core is visible throughout the ACDC spec, which repeatedly defers behavior to the EGF rather than defining it. The m-ary edge operators (`AND`, `OR`, `NAND`, `NOR`, `AVG`, `WAVG`) define provenance-chain validity, but "validity logic is EGF-dependent" (ACDC spec §Edge Section, L1112-1116). Schema availability constraints "may [be] impose[d]" by the EGF (§Schema Availability, L242). Extensibility is "permission-less... no shared governance... completely decentralized and zero-trust," and a registry of ACDC types collapses to "merely Schema discovery or schema blessing for a given context or ecosystem" (§Extensibility, L2934-2936). The recurring pattern is that the cryptographic machinery is fixed and universal while *meaning* and *fitness* are pushed out to a per-ecosystem governance layer that the core neither knows nor needs.

**The authenticity/veracity axis is the theoretical spine of this split.** Hardman's framing makes it precise: authenticity is "the truthfulness of an imputed origin"; veracity is "the truthfulness of claimed facts in content"; the two "must be analyzed primarily as orthogonal" (`authenticity-vs-veracity.md §Formal definitions`). The key doctrine is mechanizability: "Judgments about authenticity can — if managed very carefully — be reduced to an objective mathematical computation, whereas judgments about veracity inherently require subjective assessments of reputation" (`authenticity-vs-veracity.md §Independence`). Layer 1 verifies authenticity; Layer 2 (governance, reputation, human judgment) adjudicates veracity and fitness. The applied corpus states the same boundary at the level of a single credential: an ACDC validation "can prove that an issuer remains committed to a citation — but only the interaction context... plus governance, plus possible validation external to the world of ACDCs" establishes what proposition is actually true (`citation/index.md`). This is a clean statement of what cryptographic verification does and does not prove, and it is why an EGF is never optional in a real deployment even though it is invisible to the crypto.

**The governance hook is cryptographically bound, not merely referenced.** In the applied schemas the EGF is reachable by SAID: GCD (Generalized Cooperative Delegation) credentials carry a `gfw` field that "is a SAID naming a supplemental rules block," and "when defined, a verifier MUST NOT impute a delegator's approval of delegated authority to a delegate without understanding and enforcing the rules of these extra rules" (`gcd` schema `a.gfw.description`). Crucially, "the act of issuing or receiving a GCD credential constitutes binding acceptance of the rules" — a formulation that recurs verbatim across org-vet, citation, and SEDI (`gcd/index.md §Governance`). The Ricardian-contract rule section (`r`) is the general mechanism: rules are "both human and machine-readable and referenceable by a cryptographic digest," supporting the bow-tie model of Ricardian Contracts (ACDC spec §Rule Section, L1239). So an EGF's terms are not a URL a verifier optionally consults; they are a SAID-committed contract whose acceptance is entailed by participation. This is how governance binds without becoming a phone-home dependency or an external authority.

## 2. Who arbitrates ecosystem-defined behavior

The sharpest way to see KERI's governance position is by contrast with the arbiter it rejects. In the X.509/CT world, "the ultimate arbiter of CA trustworthiness is a handful of browser vendors" (`wtbo.md §1`); the root programs of Google, Mozilla, and Apple have "immediate, global effect... no formal appeals process. No government, no standards body, and no external adjudicator sits above them" (`wtbo.md` Note [b]). The load-bearing distinction is between enforcement and observation: "A browser vendor that distrusts a CA is making an enforcement decision — exercising judgment with immediate global consequences... A KERI watcher that detects duplicity is making an observation — surfacing cryptographic evidence that the relying party confirms independently" (`wtbo.md §4`). A watcher "cannot unilaterally 'distrust' an identifier the way a browser root program can distrust a CA. The worst a dishonest watcher can do is stay silent about duplicity it has seen" (`wtbo.md §4`). The architectural claim is that KERI has *no* Layer-1 arbiter at all — verification "bottoms out in mathematics," not in administrators (`wtbo.md §3`).

So who arbitrates Layer-2? The answer is deliberately plural and opt-in. The Dossier model "operates on a decentralized root of trust. A verifier does not rely on a single authority but makes explicit trust decisions about a plurality of evidence issuers" (Dossier spec §"Verifier Trust"). In a compositional dossier "trust is derived from the leaf nodes (the authorities), not merely from the dossier issuer" — the LEI issuer for legal identity, the carrier/regulator for number authority, the trademark steward for brand (Dossier spec §"Use Cases", VVP). There is no single body that certifies fitness across the ecosystem; each verifier composes trust from the specific authorities relevant to the specific claim, and the EGF for that vertical publishes the rules by which those authorities are judged. The flagship example is GLEIF's **Ecosystem Governance Framework for the vLEI**, developed with ToIP (keri.one §7, resource directory), where the arbitration structure is itself a KERI credential chain — GLEIF blesses Qualified vLEI Issuers, who vet legal entities, who authorize roles — and "A verifier receiving an employee's credential can traverse the chain back to GLEIF without consulting a trust registry. The entire graph is self-certifying" (`acdc-vc-diff.md §Real-World Validation`).

This is the direct corrective to the outsider prior that "decentralized identity needs a trust registry." KERI's position is that trust registries "are a band aid; they just move the centralization back to a different level of the architecture... immature and fragmented... primarily address governance for issuers, not issuees," and that ACDC chaining "eliminates the need for trust registries" (`x509-prob.md`). The rise of trust registries in the W3C-VC world is read as "proof that this conceptual gap needs plugging" — the gap being that a VC captures only the immediate issuer→holder edge and lets the broader chain of authority "become implicit" (`acdc-vc-diff.md §Loss 1`). The EGF plus edge-chaining fills that gap without a registry, because the fitness rules travel as SAID-committed governance and the authority chain travels as verifiable edges.

One nuance an adversarial reviewer must hold: the assurance level a verifier demands is *its own* threshold, not an ecosystem-imposed hierarchy. The org-vet schema models Level of Assurance as "a positive number, where larger numbers map to higher levels of assurance," so that "verifiers... decide what level of assurance will satisfy them, and accept any credential having an LoA >= their threshold" (`org-vet/index.md`). LoA-3 ("gold") is defined in KERI-native primitives — a ceremony proving "no MITM between any two members of the signing committee," at least one external observer, and "enough witnesses to reliably detect and recover from duplicity" — mapping to the GLEIF Legal-Entity vLEI (`org-vet/index.md`). This is a reframing of CA "high assurance" in duplicity-detection terms rather than administrative vetting, and it keeps arbitration on the verifier's side of the transaction rather than a central authority's.

## 3. SEDI: state-endorsed, not state-issued

SEDI (State-Endorsed Digital Identity) is the cleanest application of KERI's root-of-trust doctrine to civil identity, and it is worth dwelling on because it makes the governance boundary concrete against the most centralizing prior of all — the state. Its founding premise "inverts the usual state-ID relationship: identity is 'innate to the individual's existence and independent of the state... fundamental and inalienable' (§63A-20-101(1)). The state endorses an identity; it does not create one" (`sedi-id/index.md §Purpose`, quoting Utah Code). The mechanics enforce the inversion: the issuer field names the State, "but the subject is bound by the holder's own personal digital identifier (a KERI AID) — withdraw the endorsement and the identity persists, because the holder, not the state, holds the key" (`sedi-id/index.md`). This is exactly the KERI worldview — key-holder is controller, root of trust is the identifier's own key-event history (keripy-knowledge `00-lens.md` claim 1) — applied to a domain where the reflexive assumption is that the government owns the identity.

SEDI also demonstrates governance-by-schema rather than governance-by-policy-document. The statute permits endorsing "exactly four attributes — name, birth date, image, and Utah residence address," and the schema "carries those four and nothing more; everything richer... is a separate credential that chains to this one" (`sedi-id/index.md §Purpose`). Minimization is not a promise in a rulebook; it is un-satisfiable to violate because the schema decomposes name and residence into separate blinded blocks so that a verifier who needs only jurisdiction cannot force disclosure of the street line — "a single residence block would force over-disclosure on every verifier who only needs jurisdiction, making the statute's minimization duty... un-satisfiable at the schema level" (`sedi-id/index.md §Name and residence`). The privacy teeth are in the data structure. This is the schema layer doing what the governance layer specifies, and it is a template for how an EGF's rules become mechanically enforceable rather than merely aspirational.

**Duty of data loyalty** enters through SEDI's governance framework, `sedi-id/rules.json`, referenced by SAID from every SEDI credential's `r` block (SAID `EA5O9z0TB932sm8kJIVdAIpwLpEWRWC5--VNS5r69frn`). Its clauses, each grounded in Utah Code, codify a fiduciary posture toward disclosed data: `chainLinkConfidentiality` (a verifier MUST NOT "assimilate, aggregate, correlate, sell, or otherwise combine disclosed attributes... for any purpose beyond the disclosed purpose," and "this binds each downstream recipient reached through the edge section of a presentation"); `dataMinimization`; `noSurveillance` (no using a presentation "to enable monitoring, surveillance, profiling, tracking, or persistent correlation"); `respectRevocation`; `safeHarbor` (accepting the presentation elects the statutory safe harbor, and breach forfeits it and is evidence); and `noOverAssertion` (`sedi-id/index.md §Governance`). "Issuing or accepting a SEDI credential is binding acceptance of it." Data loyalty is thus a *contractual and governance* property — a duty a recipient owes because acceptance bound it to the rules — not a cryptographic guarantee. This is the essential point for the next section: the correlation-resistance a SEDI holder enjoys is assembled from a disclosure mechanism (selective disclosure of `sedi-age`), a governance duty (chain-link confidentiality, no-surveillance), and a deployment choice (un-registered one-time presentations), none of which is provided by KERI-core.

## 4. Correlation-resistance and data loyalty as layered properties

This is the section where KERI most sharply corrects a common misreading, so it must be stated flatly: **KERI-core does not promise unlinkability, and cannot.** The core is a decentralized key-state continuity and duplicity-evidence mechanism. Unlinkability, correlation-minimization, and the duty of data loyalty are properties of *higher* layers — the ACDC disclosure machinery, the governance/contractual layer, and deployment discipline — composed on top of a substrate that is itself indifferent to them. An adversarial reviewer who expects the KERI whitepaper's key-management guarantees to include privately-verifiable, correlation-free presentation has confused the layers.

The ACDC spec is explicit that even at its own layer the primary goal is not privacy: "the primary design goal is not data privacy protection per se but the more general goal of protection from the unpermissioned exploitation of data. Privacy mechanisms are means, not ends" (ACDC spec §Disclosure Mechanisms, L1659). And it is equally explicit about the *ceiling* of cryptographic unlinkability. Cryptographically-provable correlation resistance is termed **unlinkability**, but "this is a weak form... as it does not prevent statistical correlation from contextual information." "Contextual linkability may defeat cryptographic unlinkability," and "there is no cryptographic mechanism that precludes statistical correlation among a set of colluding Verifiers" (§Bulk-issued, L2785). The blunt conclusion is that advanced cryptographic unlinkability can be "an exercise in diminishing returns" without contractually-protected disclosure. This is a spec that refuses to oversell its own privacy layer.

The layering is best seen by enumerating what each stratum contributes:

- **The disclosure layer (ACDC):** the graduated-disclosure ladder — compact, metadata, partial, nested-partial, selective, full, and bulk-issued-instance disclosure — plus the private/metadata ACDC variants whose blinding UUID (`u`) forecloses rainbow reconstruction of contents from a SAID (§UUID Fields, L89-91). This layer provides *technical* correlation minimization: selective disclosure of a single blinded aggregate element "with no issuee" (`choosing-attribute-vs-aggregate.md §Caveats`), bulk-issued private ACDCs generating a fresh SAID per usage context (§Bulk-issued, L2767), and herd privacy via sparse Merkle trees so that "a given seal in the KEL... no longer provides a point of correlation to any other transaction event" (§Bulk-issued, L2914-2924). This yields "3rd party unlinkability" (§Bulk-issued, L2854).
- **The governance/contractual layer (EGF, rules):** Contractually Protected Disclosure — chain-link confidentiality and contingent disclosure — where "the disclosed data has 'strings attached'" and terms-of-use MUST propagate to each downstream Disclosee, who "in turn is the Discloser to the next" (§Contractually Protected Disclosure, L1783-1792). This is the source of "2nd party unlinkability via contractual disincentives to link" (§Bulk-issued, L2854), and it is precisely the SEDI data-loyalty duty of §3. It is a *legal/governance* control, not a cryptographic one; the spec is honest that where "Chain-link Confidentiality does not sufficiently deter provable correlation due to unpermissioned malicious collusion," you must fall back to stronger technical measures (Independent-AID or Independent-Registry bulk-issued ACDCs) "at storage/compute cost" (§Bulk-issued, L2887-2911).
- **The deployment layer:** per-facet AIDs so that "Cecilia as chief executive at Acme" and her other facets are "uncorrelatable identifiers for one person" (`sda.md §6`); frequent rotation of a citation-signing AID independent of the long-lived issuer AID; and pools of AIDs for herd privacy (Dossier spec §8 Privacy). Origin's applied doctrine makes this a hard rule: "Each facet of an identity really should have its own AID... a separate AID each time a person is granted a new Origin role... security and trust never cross-contaminate" (`managing-aliases.md §1`), correcting the surveillance-economy anti-pattern where "most of us are terribly over-identified."

The **three-party exploitation model** frames why all three layers exist: 1st party is the data subject/Discloser, 2nd party the intended Disclosee, 3rd party any non-intended Observer, and "any use of 1st party data by a 3rd party is likewise, by definition, exploitive" (§Data Privacy, L1712-1726). Third-party protection is encryption (out of scope, deferred to SPAC/TSP); second-party protection is contractual (data loyalty); and correlation-minimization across contexts is technical. The **no-forced-phone-home** property belongs to the disclosure layer and is a genuine architectural correction of OCSP/CRL: the Observer/Registrar split means Observer-to-Registrar sync happens on state changes, not at the Point of Validation, so there is "no forced phone home validation" and no forced Validator-to-Issuer correlation of ACDC usage (§TEL Registrars and TEL Observers, L1693). Contrast OCSP, which "requires the client to query a central responder for every validation, creating a privacy leak (the CA knows every site you visit)" (`wtbo.md §2`). Origin generalizes the rejection: "A registry consulted at verification time is a phone-home in disguise" (`sda.md §7`), and the SEDI portrait is committed by digest with bytes holder-carried precisely because "a resolvable URL in a credential is 'phone-home' surveillance and an availability single-point-of-failure" (`sedi-id/index.md §Image`).

The synthesis: correlation-resistance is a *stack*, and the honest KERI claim is that the stack composes cleanly — the disclosure layer minimizes technical correlators, the governance layer imposes loyalty duties on the parties the technical layer cannot reach, and deployment discipline (facet-granular AIDs, un-logged presentations) closes the remaining gaps. What KERI-core supplies is the substrate that makes each of those layers *possible* and *verifiable* — SAIDs that blind, AIDs that are cheap and disposable, anchored seals that reveal no PII, TELs that need not phone home — not unlinkability itself. A reviewer should test the seams (does the disclosure layer actually blind labels as claimed? does the governance duty actually bind downstream? does the deployment actually rotate?), not fault the base layer for a property it never claimed.

## 5. Cross-ecosystem semantics: meaning across trust domains

An ACDC is "a fragment of a globally distributed property graph" whose nodes are "securely attributed fragments... a global verifiable knowledge graph that crosses trust domains" (ACDC spec §ACDCs as secure graph fragments, L1060-1068), and "No shared or trusted relationship between the Controllers and Verifiers is REQUIRED" (§AID Fields, L95). The question is how *meaning* — not just authenticity — crosses a trust-domain boundary when there is no shared authority to define it.

KERI's first answer is **type-is-schema**: "no top-level field types exist in an ACDC... the Schema, `s`, field itself is the type field," and "type information is metadata, not data" (§Type-is-schema, L182). Because schemas are content-addressed (the `$id` is a bare SAID, not a URI, and must be static/SAIDified, §Schema ID Field Label, L186-210), the namespace *is* the content-address. This is the direct correction of the centralized-namespace prior: "no need for centralized permissioned name-space Registries to resolve name-space collisions" — the universal content-address plus content-addressable schema is the namespace, and a type registry reduces to "Schema discovery or schema blessing for a given context or ecosystem" (§Extensibility, L2936). Schemas "do not require standardization — just publication and consensus on use within an ecosystem," in pointed contrast to SD-JWT's reliance on IANA registration (`sdjwt-acdc.md §2.7`); and because a schema is referenced "by its cryptographic content, not its location," there is "no link that can rot" (`acdc-vc-diff.md §Loss 6`) — the answer to JSON-LD `@context` link-rot and term-redefinition, where "the meaning of a signed credential can be altered without invalidating the signature" (`keri-primer.md §4.1`, citing W3C vc-data-integrity issue #272).

But content-addressing a schema fixes *identity* of meaning, not *agreement* on meaning across ecosystems. Two ecosystems that mint different schema SAIDs for "residence" have two different types, and no amount of cryptography reconciles them. The applied corpus is honest about this and shows the actual technique: **semantic interop, not field-name interop.** SEDI aligns "component semantics with ISO 18013-5 mDL but keep[s] the statute's vocabulary ('residence' not 'resident')" — the interop is achieved by making the *meaning* of decomposed components match the mDL model while the field labels stay statute-native (`sedi-id/index.md §Name and residence`). Cross-ecosystem semantic agreement is a governance/standards act (align to mDL, align to ISO 6709 for geo, ISO 3166 for jurisdiction), not a protocol act. The edge `s`-constraint is the machinery that lets an ecosystem *pin* which foreign schema a chained node must satisfy: the vLEI ladder const-pins each hop's schema SAID (QVI→LE→AUTH→role), so a chain that crosses issuer trust-domains still carries a machine-checkable declaration of what each far node must be (`legal-entity-vLEI-credential.json`, `ecr-authorization-vlei-credential.json`; ACDC spec §Edge Section, L1176).

When meaning must cross into a *foreign ecosystem* entirely — W3C VC, ISO mDL, X.509, SD-JWT, AnonCreds, signed PDFs — KERI's stance is **bridge, don't adopt.** A designated bridging party verifies the foreign credential per its native rules, then issues a Foreign Artifact ACDC (FAA) or bridge-wrapper attesting "I... successfully verified the attached foreign credential on date X according to policy Y" (Dossier spec §3; `faa/index.md`). This "transforms the problem of verifying a foreign format into the problem of trusting the attestation of the bridging party" (Dossier spec §3), and the trust is explicit and issuer-scoped, never systemic: an FAA's `art_posture` records whether the issuer merely recorded a digest, witnessed the artifact, or verified its integrity, and `rev_latency` records whether and how fast it mirrors the foreign credential's revocation — "Trust in a bridge is explicit and issuer-scoped, not systemic" (`faa/index.md`). Two caveats are called out: trust depends on the bridging party's reputation/policies, and "the revocation lifecycles of the original foreign credential and of the bridge wrapper are decoupled unless a specific governance framework explicitly links them" (Dossier spec §3). This is the interop corollary of the lossless/lossy doctrine: keep the lossless ACDC authoritative and treat foreign VCs "as derivative artifacts wrapped/bridged, not as native roots of trust" (`acdc-vc-diff.md`); "AIDs can be transformed to DIDs, but the opposite transformation is typically impossible" (`sdjwt-acdc.md §2.2`), so the bridge direction matters.

The uncontested part: content-addressed static schemas eliminate the namespace-collision and link-rot problems and make type-identity cryptographic. The load-bearing assumption a reviewer should flag: *semantic* agreement across ecosystems remains a human/governance achievement, exactly as the authenticity/veracity split predicts — the protocol guarantees you are looking at schema `E...`, not that your ecosystem and mine mean the same thing by it.

## 6. Versioning and migration cost

Versioning in the KERI stack is layered like everything else, and each layer has its own compatibility rules.

**The wire/encoding layer (CESR)** versions the *code tables themselves* through a protocol genus/version code, `-_GGGVVV`, which "MUST NOT provide a count... but MUST modify the protocol genus and Version of all the following Count Codes" (CESR spec §Protocol genus/version table, L609). Genus lets one CESR serve multiple protocols each with their own tables; version lets a protocol evolve its tables (`CAA` = 2.00, `-_AAABAA` = KERI/ACDC v1.00, `-_AAACAA` = v2.00; CESR spec L615, L865-866). The backward-compatibility semantics are stated as a normative asymmetry: "Any addition of a new code to the code table is backward-breaking in at least one direction... New implementations with the new codes can accept streams from old implementations, but old ones will break if they receive the new ones" (§Protocol genus/version codes, L618). A **minor** bump = "a code is added to a table" (breaks only new-sender→old-receiver); a **major** bump = "a code's meaning changes" (breaks both directions) — with far more minor room than major (4096 minor per major) deliberately (L620-622). This is the same mechanism as **crypto agility**: a new algorithm "enters by claiming an unused CESR code-table slot; non-upgraded parsers still frame the stream correctly (read code → look up length → read bytes)" (keripy-knowledge `00-lens.md` claim 8; CESR spec §SAID, L1186). Post-quantum FN-DSA (Falcon) primitives are being introduced "in successive updates" as exactly such additive minor entries (CESR spec §Annex A, L782; §13, L1514).

**The message layer** carries a version string as its first field. v2 format `PPPPMmmGggKKKKBBBB.` encodes protocol, protocol version, CESR genus version, serialization kind, and size (CESR spec §Version String, L1137). The critical backward-compat rule: "v2 implementations MUST support v1" to verify 1.XX events (CESR spec §Legacy Version 1.XX, L1164; KERI spec L273), and the `.` terminator exists specifically to let future versions change the version-string size while preserving regex extractability (L1158). So the stack is designed for old-format ingestion by new code, not the reverse — a deliberate one-way compatibility gradient.

**The schema layer** distinguishes two "versions" that MUST NOT be confused: the `$id` SAID is "the normative determiner," "cryptographically verifiable," and used in validation; the semver `version` field is "informative" and NOT used in validation, though any content change forces a new SAID and SHOULD also bump `version` (ACDC spec §Schema Versioning, L230-234). The applied corpus operationalizes this: "$id is a SAID... Edit content → the SAID changes → registry.json and any credential referencing the old SAID must be updated. Never invent or hand-patch a SAID" (`bakobo/schema/KNOWLEDGE_TRANSFER.md`), and `registry.json` is "the crawl-free index of released schemas (SAID → path)." A schema evolution is therefore a new content-addressed object, and forward-compatibility across schema versions is handled at the edge layer, where an edge's `s`-constraint "enables forward-compatibility constraints without adding each minor version to `oneOf`" and "enables self-deprecating old schemas" (ACDC spec §Edge Section, L1176-1182).

**Migration cost is where KERI stakes its most aggressive comparative claim**, and it is worth stating carefully because it is both strong and honestly hedged. The argument is that the fair comparison between KERI and X.509 is not deployed-today capability but *migration cost*: PKI's post-quantum transition is "mandatory, global, and expensive... a global re-rooting," whereas KERI's PQ migration is "one rotation per identifier, made unilaterally... not a global re-rooting," so "early KERI adopters are not accumulating architectural debt" (`wtbo.md §6`). The grounding is that pre-rotation already hides the next key behind a hash, so a controller can "rotate to stronger quantum-safe functions over time" and remain "computationally infeasible indefinitely" (KERI spec §Pre-rotation, L1680), and that integrating a NIST-finalist PQ algorithm into keripy "took only a couple of hours" (`kspqs.md`, personal communication, Sam Smith, Oct 7 2025). The quantum posture is "hybrid ECC+PQ multisig plus the hash-hidden next key" (`00-lens.md` claim 8). This is a real architectural advantage — unilateral per-identifier migration versus coordinated global ceremony — and the honest counterweight (below) is that the *implementation* cost of getting a new code-table entry exactly right is nontrivial and error-prone.

The keripy-knowledge base is the ground truth on that implementation cost, and it is sobering. Adding a single new primitive code is "the highest-risk surface in the flagship" because a code lives in *multiple* shadow tables: registering only in `MatterCodex` + `Matter.Sizes` is not enough; digest codes must also update `Saider.Digests` and `Serder.Digests`, and the relevant `*Dex` sets (`PreDex`, `DigDex`, `SmallVrzDex`/`LargeVrzDex`), plus in 2.0 the `BodyUniversalCodex` (`BUDex`) and `Counter.BUCodes` — "Registering only in MatterCodex + Matter.Sizes is not enough and fails late/silently" (keripy-knowledge landmine L5; invariant §A9). The sizing arithmetic is unforgiving: fixed codes need `fs % 4 == 0`, variable codes `(ls + rs) % 3 == 0`, lead size `ls = (3 - (rize % 3)) % 3` where "the double-modulo is intentional (0→0, 1→2, 2→1). A 'simplification' here breaks triplet alignment" (§A1-A5), and "Get the sizing math wrong and a parser that predates your code desyncs the entire stream — silently, because CESR is self-framing" (§A intro). Pre-rotation verification can even go "permissive on a digest-code mismatch," silently excluding a mismatched `ondex` from threshold satisfaction rather than failing closed, and "New digest codes (Falcon/PQ) widen this surface" (landmine L6). Two further migration hazards live in storage: `Komer` records "have no schema versioning → adding a field breaks old stores" and "Any new field on a persisted record needs a migration" (landmine L18), and pre-1.2.0 DB key formats crash format-assuming iterators so "escrow contents [are treated] as discardable across migrations" (landmine L2; invariant I4). The lesson for a reviewer: KERI's *protocol-level* migration story (unilateral rotation, additive codes) is genuinely cheaper than PKI's, but the *implementation-level* migration surface is a minefield the reference codebase navigates with explicit shadow-table discipline, and a naive change "passes keripy's own tests but diverges from the spec/wire format" — which "is a defect, because it silently breaks interop" (keripy-knowledge `00-lens.md` claim 5).

## 7. Backward compatibility across keripy, keria, and signify-ts

The interop surface is not one implementation but at least four that must agree byte-for-byte: keripy (the Python reference), KERIA (the cloud agent), signify-ts (the TypeScript edge signer), and SignifyPy. The governing doctrine is that "keripy's 'intent layer' is exogenous: the CESR / KERI / ACDC specifications" (keripy-knowledge `00-lens.md` claim 5) — the spec is the external contract, and "CESR code-table entries and sizing, field names and order, SAID derivation, version strings, count codes are external contracts." This inverts the usual "reference implementation is the truth" assumption: passing keripy's tests is *not* sufficient; the wire format is.

The KERIA/Signify split is itself an interop architecture with a two-AID delegation spine: the Client AID (edge, delegator) and the Agent AID (cloud, delegated `dip` with `di` = Client AID), where "unencrypted private key material never leaves the client" and the agent holds only encrypted blobs it cannot decrypt (`keria/docs/protocol.md §Key Generate Methods`; `signify-ts/README.md`). Externally the agent is "just another KERI node relaying edge-signed CESR" — the Signify split is "an internal implementation detail invisible to verifiers" (`keria/README.md §Message Router`). This matters for compatibility because it means the *external* wire contract (CESR-over-HTTP on the KERI Protocol interface, port 3902) is the same normal KERI contract every implementation speaks, while the *internal* admin contract (SKRAP, the Signify/KERIA Request Authentication Protocol) is a private surface between edge and cloud.

Three compatibility surfaces are load-bearing and each has documented parity hazards:

- **HTTP message-signature construction** is "the highest-risk interop surface" — the signed byte string `ser` must be assembled identically in TS and Python or auth breaks silently. Default signed fields must match (`@method`, `@path`, `signify-resource`, `signify-timestamp`), but the two default lists are in *different order* between `authing.ts` (`@method` first) and `authing.py` (`Signify-Resource` first); order agreement is enforced by the emitted `Signature-Input` header, not the constant, "Still, this asymmetry is a landmine" (`10-keria-signify.md §7a`). Timestamp format even differs cosmetically (signify-ts writes `000+00:00` where keripy writes ISO `Z`), and both must parse each other's format.
- **Key-derivation determinism**: the passcode-to-key path must be byte-identical across edge implementations. The `bran` construction (`Salt_128 '0A' + 'A' + passcode[0:21]`), the Argon2 stretching, and the salty path must match, or "the edge and any other edge impl derive different keys from the same passcode" (`10-keria-signify.md §7c`). keripy's `SaltyCreator` must produce identical output to signify-ts `SaltyCreator` for the same salt/tier/path. The salty-path landmine (missing delimiter → seed collisions → recovery break) is an active cross-impl bug, confirmed as twin #928 (keripy-knowledge landmine L10).
- **Event/threshold/serialization parity**: the CHANGELOG parity fixes prove these are live constraints — `rotate` defaulting to next (not current) threshold (#208), correct `adds`/`cuts` (#359), string/hex `s` parsing, `ondex` computed from the *prior* establishment event not the proposed one (#378), and a `rotated=true` flag "added to match KERIpy BaseHab.sign behaviour" (`signify-ts/CHANGELOG.md` 0.4.0). Rotation-ilk switching (`drt` when delegated) and default rotation thresholds (`max(1, ceil(count/2))` in hex) must match keripy exactly (`10-keria-signify.md §7d`).

The formal compatibility contract for the admin API is machine-enforced: signify-ts "generates its request/response types directly from KERIA's OpenAPI (`npm run generate:types`)," so "KERIA's specing.py/spec.yaml is the source of truth for the admin API contract; the TS client is a downstream consumer" (`10-keria-signify.md §7e`). But the deepest layer — the CESR matter/counter/indexer code tables — is *not* generated; it is independently reimplemented in each language, "the deepest interop layer (the qb64 derivation-code tables must be identical across all four impls)" (`10-keria-signify.md` Gaps). This is the same shadow-table hazard of §6 seen from the interop side: a code added to keripy's tables but not to signify-ts's tables (or added with different sizing) is an invisible break. The honest reviewer's takeaway: backward compatibility across the stack is maintained by *convention and vigilance* (spec-as-contract, generated types where possible, CHANGELOG parity notes) rather than by a single shared codebase, and the two independent message-signature implementations plus the hand-maintained code tables are the surfaces most likely to drift.

## 8. Decentralization scorecard: KERI versus SD-JWT / OIDC4VC

The following scorecard contrasts KERI/ACDC with the SD-JWT / OIDC4VC family (SD-JWT VCs presented under OpenID4VP/OID4VCI, which build on JWT/OAuth/OIDC). A methodological caveat first, because an adversarial reviewer must weigh the source: the SD-JWT column is grounded in Hardman's comparison papers, which are KERI-partisan advocacy. The *technical* claims in it (JWT tokens verified only against current key state; `kid` points at a key not an identifier; standalone envelopes pushing toward issuer registries) are checkable against the SD-JWT and JWT specifications and are accurate as stated; the *evaluative* framing ("re-centralizes," "drift") is argumentative. Treat the ratings as a structured argument, not a neutral benchmark.

| Principle | KERI / ACDC | SD-JWT / OIDC4VC | Source grounding |
|---|---|---|---|
| Root of trust | Self-certifying identifier; "bottoms out in mathematics" | Administrative — issuer key trusted via registry/CA; bottoms out in administrators | `wtbo.md §3`; `x509-prob.md` |
| Holder key rotation | Survives; identifier constant across rotation (transferable AID) | Solved by issue-to-identifier indirection where DID method allows | `keri-primer.md §2.1`; `sdjwt-acdc.md §2.3` |
| Issuer key rotation | Credential validity binds to issuer *key state at anchor position*, survives rotation | `kid` binds to a key; "key rotation invalidates historical signatures" | ACDC spec §Binding to Key State, L1673-1675; `sdjwt-acdc.md §2.3` |
| Historical / point-in-time verification | Yes — anchored signatures defeat retrograde attacks; validate against key state "as of" the anchor | No — "tokens are only designed to be evaluated against current key state" | invariant H1; `was.md §Mitigation 3`; `sdjwt-acdc.md §2.3` |
| Trust-chain expression | Native typed, operator-bearing edges → verifiable DAG of authority | Captures only issuer→holder; broader chain "becomes implicit" | ACDC spec §Edge Section; `acdc-vc-diff.md §Loss 1` |
| Issuer-registry dependence | Eliminated by chaining; registry reduces to "schema blessing" | "SD-JWTs will need registries of trusted issuers... centralized, governed" | ACDC spec §Extensibility L2936; `sdjwt-acdc.md §2.4` |
| Revocation privacy (phone-home) | Observer/Registrar split → "no forced phone home validation" | Inherits OCSP/status-list phone-home tendencies | ACDC spec §TEL Registrars L1693; `wtbo.md §2` |
| Correlation control | Layered: graduated/selective disclosure + CLC + per-facet AIDs (see §4) | Selective disclosure at token level; weaker chain/registry-driven correlation | ACDC spec §Bulk-issued; `sdjwt-acdc.md §2.5` |
| Crypto agility / PQ migration | Additive code-table slot + unilateral per-identifier rotation | Tied to JWT/COSE alg negotiation; migration coordinated | CESR spec §SAID L1186; `wtbo.md §6` |
| Jurisdiction independence | Chains to a global root, crosses borders (vLEI, VVP) | CA/ecosystem trust is jurisdiction-bound (SHAKEN not accepted EU/Asia) | `acdc-vc-diff.md`; `keri-primer.md §1.1` |
| Canonicalization robustness | Insertion-order field maps; SAID over exact bytes; no JCS/JSON-LD | JCS "brittle"; JSON-LD term-redefinition alters signed meaning | CESR spec §Order-Preserving L1250; `keri-primer.md §4.1` |
| Offline / open-loop verification | Yes — "verify authority without consulting its issuer" | Token verification typically needs issuer metadata/keys reachable | `sda.md §7`; `sdjwt-acdc.md §2.3` |
| Ultimate arbiter | None at Layer 1; plural, opt-in EGFs at Layer 2 | Registry governance + underlying CA/browser-vendor roots | `wtbo.md §1`, §4; `x509-prob.md` |

The unifying "drift" thesis behind the scorecard is that "SD-JWT, W3C-VC, and most DID methods imported PKI/OAuth assumptions (issue-to-key thinking, current-key-state-only verification, standalone envelopes, trust registries, `@context` link-rot, three-party holder bias) and thereby re-created centralization one level up while losing time, trust-chain, and key-history fidelity" (`06-papers-di-drift.md §9`). The complementary framing is lossless-versus-lossy: ACDC is the archival RAW format and W3C-VC / SD-JWT / ISO mDL are the lossy JPEG/MP3 distribution formats, so the recommended architecture keeps the lossless ACDC authoritative and *generates* the lossy format when a consumer needs it (`acdc-vc-diff.md §Lossless vs. Lossy`).

## 9. Load-bearing assumptions and contested points

An adversarial reviewer should hold the following as the flagged uncertainties of this chapter, separated from the well-established.

**Well-established.** The two-layer split (cryptographic Layer 1 universal, semantic Layer 2 governance) is a coherent and repeatedly-stated architecture, not a rationalization (Dossier spec §"Layered verification"; ACDC spec §Extensibility). KERI-core genuinely does not promise unlinkability, and the ACDC spec is unusually honest that even its own privacy layer is defeatable by contextual/statistical correlation (§Bulk-issued, L2785). Content-addressed static schemas genuinely eliminate namespace-collision and link-rot (§Extensibility, L2936). The wire-versioning and crypto-agility mechanisms are concrete and specified (CESR spec §Protocol genus/version). The no-phone-home revocation property is real and mechanically enforced by the Observer/Registrar split.

**Contested or assumption-laden.** (1) The "no arbiter" claim is a Layer-1 claim only; at Layer 2 an EGF like GLEIF's *is* an authority, and a reviewer may fairly argue that the ecosystem's dependence on GLEIF concentrates trust even if the cryptography does not — KERI's rebuttal is that this authority is opt-in, per-vertical, and cryptographically bound rather than globally imposed, but the concentration is real. (2) The migration-cost advantage is architecturally sound at the protocol level but partly offset by a treacherous implementation-level code-table surface (keripy-knowledge landmines L5, L6, L18; invariant §A) — the "couple of hours" figure is a single data point from the protocol's own author (`kspqs.md`). (3) Cross-ecosystem *semantic* agreement is explicitly a human/governance achievement, not a protocol guarantee; the protocol fixes type-identity, not meaning-agreement, and any claim of "semantic interop" is really "we both aligned to mDL." (4) Backward compatibility across the four implementations rests on convention and vigilance (spec-as-contract, generated admin types, CHANGELOG parity) rather than shared code, with the independent message-signature implementations and hand-maintained CESR tables as the drift-prone seams (`10-keria-signify.md §7`). (5) The scorecard's SD-JWT/OIDC4VC column is sourced from KERI-partisan comparison papers; its technical claims check out, its evaluative framing is advocacy. (6) The honest external gaps apply to the whole ecosystem story: KERI's formal literature "remains thin" (the strongest KAWA analysis is a non-peer-reviewed 2025 Master's thesis), and "legal and regulatory recognition... [is] still developing... constraints of youth, not of architecture" — X.509 has eIDAS legal standing KERI lacks, "a gap of legal recognition, not of technical capability" (`wtbo.md §5-6`, Note [c]). A governance-and-ecosystem critique that lands is one that targets these seams; one that faults KERI-core for lacking unlinkability, or faults the protocol for not achieving semantic agreement, is aiming at the wrong layer.

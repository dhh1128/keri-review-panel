# 01 — Foundations and Worldview

**Thesis.** KERI (Key Event Receipt Infrastructure) is an attempt to give the internet the identity layer it was born without, and to do so by relocating the root of trust from *administrators* to *mathematics*. Its foundational move is to make an identifier self-certifying — cryptographically derived from, and controlled by, its own keys — so that the authority to speak for that identifier is proven by a replayable, hash-chained history of key events rather than asserted by any certificate authority, registry, ledger, or federation (`cr-ad-trust.md` §3-4; KERI spec §End-verifiable, L89; wtbo.md §4). Everything else in KERI's worldview follows from that single relocation: end-verifiability instead of phone-home trust; detection and recovery instead of prevention and invulnerability; local, per-identifier ordering instead of global consensus; and a three-layer stack (KERI for the "who," ACDC for the "what," CESR for the language they speak) in which each layer exists to preserve verifiability end to end (keri-primer.md §3-4). This section establishes that worldview, its precise vocabulary, the specific priors from PKI, blockchain, and mainstream verifiable-credentials work that KERI explicitly corrects, and — with equal care — the boundaries and load-bearing assumptions under which its guarantees actually hold. An adversarial reviewer should be able to leave this section knowing not just what KERI claims, but exactly where those claims are conditional.

## 1. What KERI is, and what it is not

KERI is a **decentralized key management infrastructure (DKMI)** whose purpose is to establish the authenticity of data by verifiably attributing it to a controller's identifier (KERI spec §Overlay, L69; termdefs "DKMI"). Its mission, stated in the spec, is that by exchanging key event logs each party "can validate the other's key state and securely attribute (authenticate) any signed statements or any sealed issuances of data" — this "bootstraps the use of authentic data in any interaction" (KERI spec §Direct exchange, L33). The end goal is a **trust-spanning layer for the internet**: "universal duplicity detectability and ambient verifiability with the goal of providing a universal DKMI in support of a trust-spanning layer for the internet" (KERI spec §Ecosystem, L61).

Because outsider readers reliably map KERI onto categories it does not belong to, the negative definition matters as much as the positive one. KERI is explicitly **not** the following:

- **Not a blockchain, and not dependent on global consensus.** "KERI rejects the need for global consensus regarding identity" (keri-primer.md §2). Each KEL is "somewhat like a 'blockchain' that manages the key state for one and only one AID" (KERI spec, L31), but there is no global ledger, no total ordering across identifiers, and no mining. Global total ordering of events across identifiers is an *explicit non-goal* (`background.md` §1.3). Where a ledger is ever used it is only as a last-resort tiebreaker in the extreme total-compromise case, requiring "minimal use of a distributed consensus ledger" (KERI spec §KAWA, L1888). keri.one states this as a dual property: KERI is "ledger-less... or ledger-portable" (keri.one homepage).

- **Not an encryption protocol.** "KERI is a protocol for managing identity, not for encrypting communications. This deliberate separation of concerns..." lets applications choose the best confidentiality solution independently (kspqs.md §Securing the Conversation).

- **Not a certificate scheme, and its witnesses are not CAs.** "A witness makes no such assertion... A witness simply stores and serves events" (keri-primer.md §2.6). "Witnesses make no assertion about identity — they don't claim 'this key belongs to this entity.'... That is a fundamentally different role than a CA" (wtbo.md §4).

- **Not duplicity-*resistant*, but duplicity-*evident*.** KERI does not try to make equivocation impossible — "that would require global consensus... It makes duplicity *provable and attributable*" (wtbo.md §4). This is a deliberate, load-bearing distinction, developed in depth in §4 below and in the security section of this Bible.

- **Not a prevention system.** "KERI is fundamentally a detection-oriented system with optional response mechanisms" (`background.md` §4.5). Any claim that implies prevention "must be scrutinized for hidden assumptions" (`background.md` §4.5).

- **Not, today, a drop-in replacement for all of PKI.** KERI's own advocates concede this: "This is not an argument that KERI's decentralized PKI is a drop-in replacement for classical PKI everywhere, today. It's not that simple" (wtbo.md §6). The stated constraints are "of *youth*, not of *architecture*" (wtbo.md §6) — a claim a reviewer should treat as an assertion to be tested, not a proven fact.

The unifying design ethos is **minimally sufficient means**: use "building blocks that have already been rigorously audited, formally verified, and accepted by governments and industry for years" rather than exotic cryptography (kspqs.md), and add capability in "many thin layers" following the Hourglass protocol model (KERI spec, L2940-2942). This ethos is itself a doctrinal commitment — KERI treats over-provisioned cryptographic strength as waste: "there is minimally sufficient cryptographic strength and more cryptographic strength just wastes computation and bandwidth" (CESR spec §Compact fixed-size codes, L446).

## 2. Autonomic identity and self-certifying identifiers

An **Autonomic Identifier (AID)** is "a self-managing cryptonymous identifier that must be self-certifying (self-authenticating) and must be encoded in CESR as a qualified Cryptographic primitive" (KERI spec termdefs). "Autonomic" means "self-governing, self-regulating, or self-managing" (KERI spec §AID, L111). The AID is a generalization of a **Self-Certifying Identifier (SCID)** — a cryptonym "uniquely cryptographically derived from the public key of an asymmetric keypair" (KERI spec §SCID, L107).

The self-certifying property is what removes the need for an external authority. "The identifier itself contains all the information needed to verify its authenticity. It is cryptographically derived from its controlling public keys" (kspqs.md). By contrast, "a domain name is 'rent-seeking text,' a UUID is 'arbitrary entropy'" (keri-primer.md §2.1) — neither carries any cryptographic self-evidence of who controls it.

The critical innovation over a naive SCID is **persistence across key rotation**, achieved through **transferable derivation**. A basic SCID is derived from a single public key; because the identifier *is* the key digest, changing the key means abandoning the identifier, so basic AIDs are "ephemeral" and "MUST be abandoned once the controlling private key becomes weakened or compromised" (KERI spec §SCID, L107; keri-primer.md §2.1). A transferable AID is derived instead from the entire **inception event** — the initial keys *plus* a forward commitment to the next keys — so that "the identifier string remains constant even as the keys change... Identity survives key rotation" (keri-primer.md §2.1). This is the difference between an identifier that dies with its first keypair and one that can live for decades.

This connects to a deep doctrinal claim about the nature of identity itself. In KERI's worldview, **identity means sameness**: "it's what holds constant across contexts" (x509-prob.md §Lifespans; wtbo.md §1). "An organization incorporated in 1975 doesn't renew its identity every ninety days" (`cr-ad-trust.md` §1). Privileges are dynamic and *should* expire; identity is stable and *should not*. A persistent AID is the technical embodiment of sameness — one identifier threading through every key rotation, every credential, every context — which is precisely why "renewing an *identity* is a non-sequitur" (x509-prob.md §Lifespans).

Two structural bindings make this rigorous. First, the spec's **tetrad**: for a transferable AID the protocol strongly binds four elements — the KEL, the identifier, the keypairs, and the controllers — such that "the essence of the KERI protocol is a strongly bound tetrad" that persists the identifier↔key↔controller binding across every rotation (KERI spec §Tetrad bindings, L162-182). Second, for a self-addressing inception the identifier and the event digest are literally the same value: "d and i MUST have the same value and same derivation code" (KERI spec, L1380-1381), so the identifier is cryptographically welded to the event that gave it birth.

## 3. The root of trust is the identifier's own key-event history

The **Key Event Log (KEL)** is "a Verifiable data structure that is a backward and forward chained, signed, append-only log of key events for an AID. The first entry... must be the one and only Inception event" (KERI spec termdefs). Backward chaining is by the digest of the prior event; forward chaining is the pre-rotation commitment to the next keys' digests (KERI spec, L31, L176). There are five key-event types: inception (`icp`), rotation (`rot`), interaction (`ixn`), delegated inception (`dip`), and delegated rotation (`drt`) (KERI spec §Key event, L1748). Establishment events (`icp`, `rot`, `dip`, `drt`) establish or change key state; interaction events anchor data without changing key state (KERI spec, L1751-1752).

The doctrinal heart of the entire system is where trust *bottoms out*. "This root-of-trust is cryptographic, i.e. not administrative, because it does not rely on any trusted third-party administrative process but is established with cryptographically verifiable data structures alone" (KERI spec §End-verifiable, L89). Stated as a contrast: "CT's chain of assurance bottoms out in administrators... KERI's bottoms out in mathematics: the self-certifying identifier derives from the initial public key, and the KEL's validity can be checked by anyone who replays its hash chain, without querying any authority" (wtbo.md §3).

The operational consequence is that **the head of the log *is* the current state**: "To check the current state of an AID, a verifier doesn't query a directory. They obtain the KEL and replay it. The head of the log *is* the current state, and anyone can verify it offline, at any time" (`cr-ad-trust.md` §4). There is no separate revocation infrastructure, no CRL, no OCSP responder to phone: key state — including whether an identifier has been abandoned — lives entirely in the replayable KEL and is "continuously current" (`cr-ad-trust.md` §2). Abandonment is signaled *in the log itself*: rotating to a null next-key list means the controller has abandoned the AID and "no more key events MUST be allowed in its KEL" (KERI spec, L340, L174).

Two invariants make the KEL trustworthy as a root of trust:

1. **At most one valid KEL, or none.** "In KERI, there MUST be at most one valid KEL for any identifier or none at all" (KERI spec, L178). This removes ambiguity about key state for the validator.

2. **First-seen is permanent.** "first seen, always seen, never unseen" (KERI spec §First seen, L49, L1788). This is the basis of duplicity detection: once a validator has seen a version of an event, no later, differently-signed version can silently supplant it.

A crucial and often-missed corollary: because events are signed nonrepudiably, **recovery from key compromise is not an escape from accountability**. "Because events are signed nonrepudiably, any key compromise is still the responsibility of the controller... still may be held accountable for any harm" (KERI spec §Reconciliation, L1792). Recovery repairs the KEL only for *future* validators; anyone who already first-saw the compromised events still saw them. The root of trust is a *history*, and history is not rewritten.

## 4. End-verifiability and ambient verifiability

**End-verifiability** is the property that a message's authenticity can be verified by its ultimate recipient using only the data itself, with no dependence on the security of anything in between. "Everything in KERI or that depends on KERI is also end-verifiable; therefore, KERI has no security dependency on any other infrastructure, including conventional PKI. It also does not rely on security guarantees that may or may not be provided by web or internet infrastructure" (KERI spec, L88). End-verifiability is stated as "a prerequisite for true zero-trust computing infrastructure, where zero-trust means never trust always verify" (KERI spec, L103).

**Ambient verifiability** is end-verifiability made ubiquitous: "the source of any data can be verified anywhere, at any time, by anybody. Ambient verifiability removes any need to trust any of the components in the middle, i.e., the whole internet" (KERI spec, L97). keri.one compresses this to a slogan — "Any log may be verified anywhere at anytime by anybody" (keri.one homepage).

The strategic payoff of ambient verifiability is a **security asymmetry favoring the defender**. Because verifiable copies of a KEL percolate everywhere, an attacker attempting to rewrite the past must isolate a validator from all ambient sources — and "isolation... may be prohibitively expensive. Consequently, ambient verifiability provides asymmetry between the attacker and the defender in favor of the defender" (KERI spec, L1884). This underwrites the broader thesis that "if the edges of the network are secure, then the security of the middle does not matter," because "protecting one's private keys is much easier than protecting all internet infrastructure" (KERI spec, L99). keri.one puts the same thesis in one line: "Much easier to secure one's own keys well than to secure everyone else's internet computing infrastructure well" (keri.one homepage).

End-verifiability also drives KERI's rejection of **phone-home** verification. A verification model that consults the issuer (or a registry, or a status endpoint) at verification time reintroduces exactly the runtime dependency end-verifiability exists to eliminate. "A registry consulted at verification time is a phone-home in disguise" (`sda.md` §7); "verification must succeed from the credential and key-state alone, and any registry or log is an issuance-time and audit-time convenience, never a verification-time dependency" (`sda.md` §7). The ACDC spec builds this into its architecture: the Observer/Registrar split means an ACDC's revocation state can be checked "without exposing a point of validation (PoV)" — "no forced phone home validation" (ACDC spec §TEL Registrars and Observers, L1693). This is a direct, named rejection of the OCSP/CRL model.

**Discovery is separated from trust.** KERI still needs a way to *find* an identifier's KEL, and it uses **Out-Of-Band Introductions (OOBIs)** — a URI associated with an AID. But an OOBI is deliberately untrusted: "an OOBI itself is not trusted but MUST be verified... any information obtained from the service endpoint provided in the OOBI MUST be verified by some other mechanism" (KERI spec, L2629); "a KERI OOBI by itself is considered insecure with respect to KERI" (KERI spec, L2633). Because whatever an OOBI delivers is itself end-verifiable, the discovery channel does not need to be trusted — so KERI "does not, therefore, need its own dedicated discovery network; OOBIs with URLs will do," and may "safely use DNS/CA, web search engines, social media, email, and messaging as discovery mechanisms. The worst case is the OOBI fails" (KERI spec, L2635, L2942). This is the sharp line KERI draws that most systems blur: **finding data is untrusted; verifying it is self-contained.**

## 5. The three-layer stack: KERI, CESR, ACDC — and why each exists

KERI's worldview is realized as a three-layer stack with a clean division of labor: **KERI is the "Who" (identity), ACDC is the "What" (data), and CESR is "the language they speak"** (keri-primer.md §3-4). Each layer exists because a specific property could not be obtained otherwise.

### 5.1 KERI — the identity / key-management layer

KERI answers *who controls this identifier, right now, and how do I know?* It contributes the AID, the KEL, pre-rotation, witnesses, delegation, and the duplicity model. It is the root-of-trust layer; without it, the layers above have nothing to attribute data *to*. Its most important security contribution — pre-rotation as a firewall between everyday signing authority and the authority to rotate keys — is treated at length in the security sections of this Bible; here it suffices to note that pre-rotation is what makes an AID *survivable*: "even if a hacker can abuse a current key, they can't rotate to new keys and take permanent control without accomplishing a second hack somewhere else" (x509-prob.md §Prerotation).

### 5.2 CESR — the encoding / interchange layer

**CESR (Composable Event Streaming Representation)** is "a dual text-binary encoding format that has the unique property of text-binary concatenation composability" (CESR spec §SAD Path Signatures, L1396). It exists to make cryptographic material streamable, self-framing, and losslessly convertible between a human-readable text domain and a compact binary domain, en masse, without ambiguity. Its defining invariant is **composability**: "any set of Self-Framing concatenated Primitives expressed in either the Text domain or Binary domain may be converted as a group to the other Domain and back again without loss" (CESR spec §Composability, L5). Naive Base64, by padding each primitive independently, provides only "one-way composability" and so is explicitly rejected (CESR spec, L252).

CESR "was developed for the KERI protocol" (CESR spec §SAID, L1186) but is "not limited to merely encoding Cryptographic Primitives" — any primary datatype may be encoded (CESR spec §Abstract Domain, L11). It exists as a distinct layer for three reasons a reviewer should weigh:

- **Bandwidth and edge devices.** Compact one/two-character type codes minimize over-the-wire signed bytes for resource-constrained (IoT, supply-chain) applications; a verbose semantic overlay can be applied *after* verification (KERI spec §Compact labels, L257; ACDC spec §Compact Labels, L58).
- **Crypto agility without stream desynchronization.** A derivation/type code names the crypto suite; a new algorithm claims a previously-unused code point, and "old ones will break if they receive the new ones" while new implementations still accept old streams (CESR spec §Protocol genus/version codes, L618). "The CESR derivation code enables cryptographic digest algorithm agility... This provides interoperable future-proofing" (CESR spec §SAID, L1186). This is the mechanism behind KERI's post-quantum migration story: a new signature scheme is "an event in the KEL. Not a global infrastructure project" (`cr-ad-trust.md` §6).
- **Deterministic parsing of interleaved serializations.** A CESR parser must support JSON, CBOR, and MGPK interleaved at the top level, distinguished by mutually-distinct start bits — "Each Stream MUST start (restart) with one of eight cases" (CESR spec §Stream parsing rules, L427).

### 5.3 ACDC — the authentic-data / credential layer

**ACDC (Authentic Chained Data Container)** is the data layer: an ordered nested field map, every block of which carries a SAID, making it a Self-Addressed Data structure (ACDC spec §ACDC Message Fields, L2982). It is "issued by" and "issued to" AIDs (ACDC spec §Targeted Attribute Section, L318), so authentic data is always attributable to a KERI-rooted controller. ACDCs are "fragments of a globally distributed property graph" — connected by edges into "a global verifiable knowledge graph that crosses trust domains" (ACDC spec §ACDCs as secure graph fragments, L1060-1068), and crucially "No shared or trusted relationship between the Controllers and Verifiers is REQUIRED" (ACDC spec §AID Fields, L95).

ACDC is deliberately more than a format. "ACDCs are not just a format — they're a *methodology* for creating verifiable evidence" (x509-prob.md §Much better alternatives). The central metaphor for its role in the stack is **lossless vs. lossy**: ACDC is the RAW/FLAC archival source of truth, while W3C-VC, SD-JWT, and ISO mDL are the JPEG/MP3 lossy distribution formats — "once you've chosen a lossy format as your primary evidence, you cannot reconstruct what you've lost" (acdc-vc-diff.md §Lossless vs. Lossy). The recommended posture is to keep the lossless ACDC authoritative and *generate* lossy formats from it when a particular ecosystem requires them (acdc-vc-diff.md §The Right Format for the Job).

### 5.4 SAID — the connective tissue

Binding all three layers is the **Self-Addressing Identifier (SAID)**: "a special type of content-addressable identifier based on an encoded cryptographic digest that is self-referential" — embedded *inside* the very serialization it identifies (CESR spec §SAID, L1184). Its foundational claim: "Any sufficiently strong cryptographic commitment to a content-addressable identifier is functionally equivalent to a cryptographic commitment to the content itself" (CESR spec §SAID, L1188). A signature on a SAID therefore commits to the entire serialization it summarizes (KERI spec, L306; ACDC spec §SAID Fields, L81). This is why "An ACDC is not a mutable document; it is a crystallized fact" (keri-primer.md §3.2): "a SAID will verify if and only if its encompassing serialization has not been mutated, which makes the content immutable" (CESR spec §SAID, L1196). SAIDs are what let KELs anchor arbitrary data, let ACDCs reference each other by content across trust domains, and let CESR carry compact commitments to large structures — the same primitive doing structural work at every layer.

A note a reviewer should carry forward: KERI/ACDC canonicalization uses **insertion order, not lexicographic order**. "The natural canonical ordering for such mappings is insertion order" (CESR spec §Order-Preserving Data Structures, L1250); lexicographic (JCS-style) canonicalization is called "un-natural" and rejected because it forces artificially-named fields to coerce a logical order (CESR spec, L1250-1256). This is a pointed correction of a mainstream verifiable-credentials assumption, examined in §7.

## 6. The X.509 / PKI problem KERI answers

KERI's positive design is best understood as a set of answers to specific, enumerated flaws in the incumbent PKI/X.509 model. The spec, the papers, and the security-analysis corpus converge on the same diagnosis.

**The core flaw is separation of the identifier from its keys.** In X.509 an identifier "is a record in a database. The key is a separate artifact. The binding between them is an assertion" — a certificate signed by a third party. "This places the root of trust outside the identity itself" (`cr-ad-trust.md` §1). The KERI spec states the same flaw as verifiability failure: "the mapping between the identifier (domain name) and the controlling keypair(s) is merely asserted by a trusted entity (a certificate authority or CA)... a Verifier cannot verify cryptographically the mapping... but must trust the operational processes of the CA" — and the cert is signed "with keypairs controlled by the CA," giving "evidence of authenticity of the assignment... but not evidence of the veracity of the mapping" (KERI spec §Overcoming existing security overlay flaws, L81).

**The second flaw is no cryptographic link across rotation.** "When rotating the valid signing keys, there is no cryptographically Verifiable way to link the new (rotated in) controlling/signing key(s) to the prior (rotated out)" (KERI spec, L83). CRLs (RFC 5280) and OCSP (RFC 6960) do not fix this — "This merely is asserted." Rotation by re-issuing a certificate "is essentially the same as starting over by creating a brand-new independent mapping" (KERI spec, L83), which the spec speculates "could well be one of the main reasons that other key assignment methods, such as PGP's web-of-trust failed." A pointed real-world tell: Google Cloud in 2026 advised customers *not* to pin certificates because pinning breaks during routine rotation — proof "there is no cryptographic thread joining the old key to the new one, so a relying party that pinned the old key pinned nothing durable" (x509-prob.md §Prerotation; wtbo.md §1). KERI's pre-rotation is exactly what would let a relying party commit to a durable cryptographic anchor *and* survive rotation.

Further enumerated PKI failures the corpus corrects:

- **The wrong locus of risk.** "*Certificate authorities aren't the ones that choose or manage the keys in certs.* Holders of certs do that" (x509-prob.md §Governance). The ubiquitous risk lives in *holders'* key management, which is opaque and unauditable — the "missile silo" guarded by "one opaquely managed secret" (x509-prob.md §Governance).
- **Identity evidence that expires by design.** X.509's mandatory Validity field means "Evidence of identity expires by design, not by necessity" (`cr-ad-trust.md` §1), and — perversely — the churn "created a strong incentive to take the insecure and easy path: if there's a risk of a compromised key, just wait until the cert expires" (x509-prob.md §Revocation is disincented). "The organizations most in need of urgent revocation are the ones most likely to wait" (`cr-ad-trust.md` §1).
- **Fail-open revocation.** When an OCSP responder is offline, browsers "accept the unverifiable certificate as valid because blocking traffic is considered too disruptive" — so "The system is most vulnerable precisely when it is most needed" (keri-primer.md §1.1; wtbo.md §2).
- **Certificate Transparency redistributes trust rather than eliminating it.** CT "is a detection mechanism, not a prevention mechanism" (keri-primer.md §1.1), with a Maximum Merge Delay latency window, a privacy cost on its gossip protocol, and an ultimate arbiter that is "a handful of browser vendors" (wtbo.md §1) with "immediate, global effect... no formal appeals process" (wtbo.md Note [b]). "The result is not a decentralized trust hierarchy. It is a more elaborate one" (`cr-ad-trust.md` §2).
- **Jurisdiction-bound CA trust.** "Assertions rooted in the SHAKEN ecosystem mandated by US regulators are not accepted in Europe or Asia" (keri-primer.md §1.1). KERI crosses jurisdictions because it needs no CA and no central store.

**The retrograde attack — the deepest contribution.** The paper *Why Anchored Signatures?* argues that bare digital signatures are "much weaker evidence than casual thinkers might imagine" because they are "difficult to sequence relative to a compromise or revocation event" (was.md intro). If an attacker *ever* obtains a key, they can forge evidence "that looks like it originated in the past, *forever*" — and this remains possible "even if she rotates or revokes K before Malfoy steals it" (was.md §Retrograde attack). The backdating window has no endpoint. X.509 "embrace[s] this limitation — you can ask whether one is valid *now*... but you can't ask whether it was valid a week ago" (was.md §Mitigation 3), a limitation that also infects JWTs, OAuth2, OIDC, SD-JWTs, and DIDs that resolve only to current key state. NIST SP 800-102 is cited in agreement: a signed message "provides no assurance that the private key was used to sign the message at that time" (keri-primer.md §3.3). KERI's answer is **anchored signatures**: "Keep tamper-evident records that can prove how a given signing event relates in time to changes in the associated key state" (was.md §Solution) — implemented by anchoring the hashes of signed events in the KEL (and TELs anchored in the KEL). Once a key is rotated, "Malfoy's attack window closes forever" (was.md §Solution). Notably, this requires only per-identifier ordering, "no total ordering, and no cumbersome consensus algorithm" (was.md §Solution) — which is why it does *not* require a blockchain.

One honest boundary the corpus itself states: this is a *migration-cost* argument, not a claim of present-day parity. X.509 has legal standing (eIDAS) KERI lacks — "a gap of legal recognition, not of technical capability" (wtbo.md Note [c]) — and "KERI's oft-cited operational complexity is itself load-bearing" (wtbo.md §6). A reviewer should hold KERI to that framing: the claim is that KERI's constraints are of immaturity while PKI's are architectural (`cr-ad-trust.md` §6), and that is a falsifiable, contestable position.

## 7. The decentralization-purity thesis and where the mainstream diverged

KERI's corpus advances a strong historical claim: there is a lineage of "identity evidence" generations, and the mainstream took a wrong turn partway through. X.509/PKI is generation 1.0; DIDs and W3C Verifiable Credentials are 2.0 ("next-generation X.509," genuinely praised but half-finished); ACDC/KERI is 3.0, "in production since December 2022, when the first global-scale systems went to production" (x509-prob.md). The "drift" critique is that SD-JWT, W3C-VC, and most DID methods imported PKI/OAuth assumptions and thereby "re-created centralization one level up while losing time, trust-chain, and key-history fidelity" (`06-papers-di-drift.md` §9). The author is careful to note he "worked on both of these standards, and I remain proud of their virtues" (x509-prob.md §Much better alternatives) — this is an insider's critique, not an outsider's dismissal.

The **purity position**, distilled: trust must be *cryptographic and local*, not *administrative and global*; verification must be *offline, open-loop, and observer-independent*; security is *survivable and duplicity-evident* (detect + recover), never claimed as prevented; and every guarantee is stated against explicit assumptions with honest scope limits (`06-papers-di-drift.md` §9).

The specific divergences the corpus identifies:

**DIDs solved holder rotation, then stalled on issuer rotation.** Issuing evidence *to* an identifier rather than *to* a key solves rotation for the holder. "But there's a hitch. Evidence is also *issued by* an identifier... If the issuer updates their identifier, old evidence is invalidated" — and "This risk is poorly understood in SSI circles" (x509-prob.md §Much better alternatives). The vivid framing: a driver's-license bureau "whose recover-from-breach plan required all current license holders to come back to the office" (was.md).

**Most DIDs resolve only to current key state.** "*Most DIDs, including crowd favorites like did:web, can only be resolved to the current key state*" (was.md §Mitigation 3), so historical actions cannot be verified unless the method supports `versionId`/`versionTime` — which most do not. This makes DIDs vulnerable to the retrograde attack of §6. (Note also: "AIDs can be transformed to DIDs, but the opposite transformation is typically impossible" — sdjwt-acdc.md §2.2 — because a DID generally lacks KERI's key-history substrate to reconstruct.)

**W3C VCs are lossy, in six enumerated ways** (acdc-vc-diff.md): (1) the *trust chain* collapses to a single issuer→holder hop, with the broader chain going implicit — "the rise of trust registries is proof that this conceptual gap needs plugging"; (2) *time* — "W3C VCs are generally verifiable only in the present"; (3) *edge weights / conditional logic* — RDF triples cannot attach properties to edges, forcing reification and "triple bloat," whereas ACDC edges are native property-graph objects with `w`/`o`/`s`/`n` fields; (4) *use cases beyond credentials* — VCs assume the three-party issuer→holder→verifier model, so "For pure attestation, VCs are the wrong shape"; (5) *key-management transparency* — a VC issuer's key history is opaque; (6) *schema stability* — `@context` URLs suffer link rot, and JSON-LD term redefinition can change a signed credential's meaning "without breaking the cryptographic signature."

**SD-JWT re-centralizes and signs the wrong thing.** SD-JWTs are standalone envelopes that "will need registries of trusted issuers... Such registries will need to be centralized, governed, and managed for scale" (sdjwt-acdc.md §2.4), whereas ACDCs "prove the bona fides of issuers by chaining to other ACDCs." SD-JWT's `kid` "points at the key, not the identifier, so key rotation invalidates historical signatures" (sdjwt-acdc.md §2.3); its `iat` is self-asserted and "tokens are only designed to be evaluated against current key state" (sdjwt-acdc.md §2.3). SD-JWT's flexibility in identifier type is reframed as a false virtue: it "makes it much more difficult to predict or enforce security properties" (sdjwt-acdc.md §2.2), whereas ACDC mandates that issuers be AIDs.

**Trust registries are a band-aid.** "These are a band aid; they just move the centralization back to a different level of the architecture... immature and fragmented... primarily address governance for issuers, not issuees" (x509-prob.md). ACDC chaining "eliminates the need for trust registries" by letting a verifier "traverse the chain back to [the root] without consulting a trust registry. The entire graph is self-certifying" (acdc-vc-diff.md §Real-World Validation, describing the GLEIF vLEI graph).

**Canonicalization and the JSON-LD trap.** `{"a":1,"b":2}` and `{"b":2,"a":1}` are "semantically identical but have different cryptographic hashes"; JCS (RFC 8785) normalization "is brittle," and JSON-LD term redefinition means "the meaning of a signed credential can be altered without invalidating the signature" (keri-primer.md §4.1, citing W3C vc-data-integrity issue #272). KERI/ACDC's answer is insertion-ordered field maps plus SAIDs over exact byte serializations — the correction described in §5.4.

**"Sounds like blockchain" is a lazy tell.** The corpus repeatedly corrects the reflex that decentralized identity needs a chain. Anchoring needs only per-identifier ordering: "no permissioning problem, no big central *anything* in the sky, no scale or performance bottlenecks, and no regulatory problems with data locality, privacy, or erasure" (was.md §Solution). Crucially for privacy law: to forget an identifier on a blockchain "requires a hard fork of the entire chain," whereas KERI's non-intertwined microledgers let a user delete a specific KEL "without disrupting the global ecosystem" (keri-primer.md §3.5; keri.one homepage — "data may be erased and truly forgotten").

Underpinning all of this is one architectural fork the corpus names explicitly: **open-loop vs. closed-loop verification**. In closed-loop (object-capability) systems "the party that *issues* authority is the same party that *checks* it"; in open-loop (verifiable-credential) systems "a third party who is *not* the issuer can verify authority *without* consulting the issuer... Authority is analyzed by a stranger" (`sda.md` §7). KERI/ACDC is committed to the open-loop model — which is the same commitment, seen from the authority angle, that end-verifiability makes from the data angle. The mainstream's drift toward registries, status endpoints, and current-key-state-only resolution is, in this framing, a quiet slide back toward closed-loop, phone-home trust.

A final worldview distinction that governs how far cryptography can even reach: **authenticity vs. veracity.** Authenticity is "truthfulness of an imputed origin"; veracity is "truthfulness of claimed facts in content"; they are "primarily... orthogonal" (authenticity-vs-veracity.md). The load-bearing claim is that "Judgments about authenticity can — *if managed very carefully* — be reduced to an objective mathematical computation, whereas judgments about veracity inherently require subjective assessments of reputation" (authenticity-vs-veracity.md). KERI/ACDC mechanizes authenticity and deliberately does *not* claim to mechanize veracity — a scope discipline the applied schema corpus restates bluntly: an ACDC's validation "can prove that an issuer remains committed to a citation — but only the interaction context... plus governance... establishes what proposition is true" (`citation/index.md`). This is why the doctrine insists a human keeps the final trust judgment: "Automate the introduction. Automate the proof where you can. But never automate the verification of the proof" (`oia.md` §Connections).

## 8. Well-established vs. contested: the load-bearing assumptions

A doctrine reference for adversarial reviewers must separate what is settled from what is asserted. The following is the honest ledger the corpus itself supplies.

**Well-established (definitional or cryptographically grounded):**

- The self-certifying derivation, the KEL structure, pre-rotation, and SAID/anchoring mechanics are specified and implemented; their correctness rests on standard assumptions — hash and signature security ("A1 — Cryptographic Soundness," `background.md` §4.6) and ~128-bit strength targets (KERI spec Annex A, L1698-1704; CESR spec §Compact fixed-size codes, L446).
- KERI's survivability/detection orientation is a *stated design goal*, not an accident. "KERI, as specified, adopts a survivability-oriented security model" (`standard-analysis.md` §0); it "is explicitly **not** designed around invulnerability" (`background.md` §4.5.1). Criticizing KERI for not *preventing* malicious-controller duplicity is a category error — "analogous to criticizing a smoke detector for not preventing fires" (`dg-c02-claude.md` §5).
- The post-quantum argument for pre-rotation is sound in its narrow form: hiding the next key behind a hash means a quantum attacker "must first invert the digest... Pre-quantum cryptographic strength is, therefore, not weakened post-quantum" (CESR spec §13, L1510-1512; KERI spec, L1711-1713), because Grover gives only a quadratic speedup against hashes while Shor gives an exponential one against RSA/ECC (`cr-ad-trust.md` §3).

**Contested, conditional, or load-bearing assumptions a reviewer must not let pass silently:**

- **KERI does not guarantee global convergence.** "Permanent divergence is an allowed outcome" (`background.md` §7.4.4). Safety means validators do not accept *invalid* histories — it does *not* mean they agree on a single history (`background.md` §7.5.1). Different validators may accept different valid KELs indefinitely with no protocol-level resolution (DG-C03).
- **KAWA's strongest property is conditional on an honest controller.** Its consistency property (no two correct witnesses accept conflicting events at the same sequence number) "does not hold if the controller is malicious" (`background.md` §5.6, P2). A malicious controller "can simply run its own witnesses" (thesis Ch.3, in `background.md` §8.2), so witness thresholding alone does not bind an attacker who controls the witnesses. The honest-controller premise is "a proof technique assumption... not a KERI deployment assumption" (`dg-c06-claude.md` §3) — removing it "shifts the focus from prevention to detection and recovery" — but that shift *depends on the observer layer actually existing*.
- **The observer layer (watchers, jurors, judges) is optional and unenforced.** "None of A8-A11 are enforced by KERI" (`background.md` §6.4); observer presence "MUST NOT be silently assumed" (A5, `background.md` §4.6). "Observers increase *visibility*, not *control*" (`background.md` §6.6). This is the genuine, surviving residual criticism: detection is delegated to an optional layer whose tooling is still maturing — "a critique of *deployment readiness and ecosystem maturity*, not of KERI's protocol design" (`dg-c02-claude.md` §6). The blunt operational statement: "A KERI deployment without robust watchers and governance is not a secure system" (`cr-ad-trust.md` §5).
- **Practical mitigation may pull toward centralization.** The "super watcher" archival pattern gives broad coverage but "reintroduces a centralized trust dependency that KERI was designed to avoid" (`cr-ad-trust.md` §5; DG-C05). KERI's architects recommend the *local-resolver* pattern as the default precisely to resist this (wtbo.md §5), but market pressure toward centralized resolution is a real, observed force (`dg-c04-claude.md` §7.5).
- **Catastrophic double-compromise is unrecoverable by design.** If an attacker captures both the current keys *and* the pre-committed rotation keys, "no cryptographic mechanism can restore control. The identifier must be abandoned" (wtbo.md §5). The corpus frames this honestly as "genuine operational pain" that "is also the *only* recovery mode X.509 supports for *any* key compromise" (wtbo.md §5) — a fair point, but one a reviewer should weigh rather than accept as settled.
- **The formal literature is thin.** KERI's most rigorous formal analysis is a 2025 ETH Zürich Master's thesis (Del Giudice), which is "not peer-reviewed" (wtbo.md §5; `cr-ad-trust.md` §5). Every "weakness" that thesis surfaces resolves, on analysis, to a deliberate non-goal, a proof-scope limitation, or a deployment-maturity gap (`07-security-analysis.md` §8 meta-pattern) — but that resolution is itself an argument the corpus makes, not an independently established consensus.

The disciplined stance the corpus models — and that this Bible adopts — is that **security properties must always be stated relative to their assumptions**: "Agreement thresholds do not imply global safety... Failure to state these boundaries results in overstated guarantees" (`background.md` §4.7, §5.10). KERI's worldview is coherent and, on its own survivability-oriented terms, internally consistent. The legitimate open questions are not about the protocol's logic but about whether the surrounding ecosystem — watchers, governance, legal recognition, tooling — matures fast enough to deliver in practice the detection-and-recovery that the design *permits* but does not *compel*.

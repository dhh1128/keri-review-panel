# 01 — Foundations and Worldview {#foundations}

## Thesis {#thesis}

KERI (Key Event Receipt Infrastructure) tries to give the internet the identity layer it was born without by relocating the root of trust from administrators to mathematics. Its foundational move is to make an identifier self-certifying — derived from, and controlled by, its own keys — so that the authority to speak for it is proven by a replayable, hash-chained history of key events rather than asserted by a certificate authority, registry, ledger or federation (KERI spec > End-verifiable, L89; papers `wtbo.md` > 4. KERI's Architecture: A Different Root of Trust). The rest of the worldview follows from that relocation: end-verifiability instead of phone-home trust; detection and recovery instead of prevention; per-identifier ordering instead of global consensus; and a three-layer stack in which KERI is the "who," ACDC the "what," and CESR the language they speak (papers `keri-primer.md` > 3. ACDCs; 4. CESR). This chapter states that worldview, the priors from PKI, blockchain and mainstream verifiable-credentials work that it corrects, and the assumptions under which its guarantees hold.

## 1. What KERI is, and what it is not {#what-keri-is}

KERI is a decentralized key management infrastructure (DKMI) whose purpose is to establish the authenticity of data by verifiably attributing it to a controller's identifier (KERI spec > Overlay, L69). By exchanging key event logs each party "can validate the other's key state and securely attribute (authenticate) any signed statements or any sealed issuances of data" (KERI spec > Direct exchange, L33), with the goal of "universal duplicity detectability and ambient verifiability with the goal of providing a universal DKMI in support of a trust-spanning layer for the internet" (KERI spec > Ecosystem, L61).

Outsiders reliably map KERI onto categories it does not belong to, so the negative definition matters as much.

- Not a blockchain. "KERI rejects the need for global consensus regarding identity" (papers `keri-primer.md` > 2. KERI). Each KEL is "somewhat like a 'blockchain' that manages the key state for one and only one AID" (KERI spec, L31), with no global ledger and no ordering across identifiers, which is an explicit non-goal (keri-security-analysis `background.md` > 1.3 Explicit Non-Goals). A ledger appears only as a last-resort tiebreaker after total compromise, "minimal use of a distributed consensus ledger" (KERI spec > KAWA, L1888); KERI is "ledger-less... or ledger-portable" (keri.one homepage).
- Not an encryption protocol: "KERI is a protocol for managing identity, not for encrypting communications." (papers `kspqs.md` > Securing the Conversation: Addressing HNDL); [chapter 12](12-confidentiality-and-secure-messaging.md#confidentiality) covers confidentiality.
- Not a certificate scheme: "Witnesses make no assertion about identity — they don't claim 'this key belongs to this entity.'... That is a fundamentally different role than a CA" (papers `wtbo.md` > 4. KERI's Architecture: A Different Root of Trust).
- Not duplicity-resistant but duplicity-evident: KERI does not make equivocation impossible, which "would require global consensus... It makes duplicity *provable and attributable*" (same) ([chapter 02, section 3](02-security-model-and-threat-posture.md#duplicity-evident)).
- Not a prevention system: "KERI is fundamentally a detection-oriented system with optional response mechanisms" (keri-security-analysis `background.md` > 4.5 Detection vs Prevention), and any claim implying prevention "must be scrutinized for hidden assumptions" (same).
- Not, today, a drop-in replacement for PKI: "This is not an argument that KERI's decentralized PKI is a drop-in replacement for classical PKI everywhere, today. It's not that simple" (papers `wtbo.md` > 6. The Transition Problem: A Comparison of Trajectories). Its constraints are said to be "of *youth*, not of *architecture*" (same), an assertion to test, not a proven fact.

The design ethos is minimally sufficient means: "building blocks that have already been rigorously audited, formally verified, and accepted by governments and industry for years" (papers `kspqs.md`), added in "many thin layers" on the hourglass model (KERI spec > OKEA, L2952). Over-provisioned strength is waste: "there is minimally sufficient cryptographic strength and more cryptographic strength just wastes computation and bandwidth" (CESR spec > Compact fixed-size codes, L446).

## 2. Autonomic identity and self-certifying identifiers {#autonomic-identity}

An Autonomic Identifier (AID) is "a self-managing cryptonymous identifier that must be self-certifying (self-authenticating) and must be encoded in CESR as a qualified Cryptographic primitive" (KERI spec termdefs). It generalizes the Self-Certifying Identifier (SCID), a cryptonym "uniquely cryptographically derived from the public key of an asymmetric keypair" (KERI spec > SCID, L107). Self-certification removes the external authority: "The identifier itself contains all the information needed to verify its authenticity. It is cryptographically derived from its controlling public keys" (papers `kspqs.md`), whereas "a domain name is 'rent-seeking text,' a UUID is 'arbitrary entropy'" (papers `keri-primer.md` > 2.1 Autonomic identifiers).

The innovation over a bare SCID is persistence across rotation. A basic AID is the key digest and "MUST be abandoned once the controlling private key becomes weakened or compromised" (KERI spec > SCID, L107). A transferable AID is derived from the whole inception event, including a commitment to the next keys, so "the identifier string remains constant even as the keys change... Identity survives key rotation" (papers `keri-primer.md` > 2.1 Autonomic identifiers). [Chapter 03, sections 2 and 3](03-key-management-and-identifier-lifecycle.md#inception) own the mechanics, including the tetrad binding and the `d == i` rule.

Behind this is a claim about identity itself: identity is sameness, "it's what holds constant across contexts" (papers `x509-prob.md` > Lifespans don't match). "An organization incorporated in 1975 doesn't renew its identity every ninety days" (keri-security-analysis `cr-ad-trust.md` > 1. The Identity Problem: Administrative Trust and Its Limits). Privileges are dynamic and should expire; identity is stable and should not, which is why "renewing an *identity* is a non-sequitur" (same). A persistent AID is sameness made technical.

## 3. The root of trust is the identifier's own key-event history {#root-of-trust}

The KEL is a backward- and forward-chained, signed, append-only log of an AID's key events ([chapter 03, section 1](03-key-management-and-identifier-lifecycle.md#kel-event-types)). Trust bottoms out there: "This root-of-trust is cryptographic, i.e. not administrative, because it does not rely on any trusted third-party administrative process but is established with cryptographically verifiable data structures alone" (KERI spec > End-verifiable, L89). In contrast, "CT's chain of assurance bottoms out in administrators... KERI's bottoms out in mathematics: the self-certifying identifier derives from the initial public key, and the KEL's validity can be checked by anyone who replays its hash chain, without querying any authority" (papers `wtbo.md` > 3. Common Ground: What CT and KERI Share).

"The head of the log *is* the current state, and anyone can verify it offline, at any time" (keri-security-analysis `cr-ad-trust.md` > 4. KERI's Architecture: A Different Root of Trust). There is no separate revocation infrastructure: key state, including abandonment, lives in the replayable KEL, which is "continuously current" (keri-security-analysis `cr-ad-trust.md` > 2. Certificate Transparency: Progress and Its Limits).

Two invariants make the KEL a root of trust. "In KERI, there MUST be at most one valid KEL for any identifier or none at all" (KERI spec, L178), and events are "first seen, always seen, never unseen" (KERI spec > First seen, L49, L1788), which is the basis of duplicity detection. Because events are signed nonrepudiably, recovery from compromise is not an escape from accountability ([chapter 03, section 10](03-key-management-and-identifier-lifecycle.md#recovery-rotation)). The root of trust is a history, and history is not rewritten.

## 4. End-verifiability and ambient verifiability {#end-verifiability}

### End-verifiability {#end-verifiability-defined}

A message's authenticity can be verified by its ultimate recipient from the data alone, with no dependence on anything in between: "Everything in KERI or that depends on KERI is also end-verifiable; therefore, KERI has no security dependency on any other infrastructure, including conventional PKI." (KERI spec, L88). It is "a prerequisite for true zero-trust computing infrastructure, where zero-trust means never trust always verify" (KERI spec, L103).

#### Ambient verifiability {#ambient-verifiability}

Ambient verifiability makes it ubiquitous: "the source of any data can be verified anywhere, at any time, by anybody. Ambient verifiability removes any need to trust any of the components in the middle, i.e., the whole internet" (KERI spec, L97). Because verifiable copies of a KEL percolate everywhere, an attacker rewriting the past must isolate a validator from all of them, and "isolation... may be prohibitively expensive. Consequently, ambient verifiability provides asymmetry between the attacker and the defender in favor of the defender" (KERI spec, L1884). Hence "protecting one's private keys is much easier than protecting all internet infrastructure" (KERI spec, L99).

End-verifiability is also why KERI rejects phone-home verification: consulting an issuer, registry or status endpoint at verification time reintroduces the dependency it exists to remove. "A registry consulted at verification time is a phone-home in disguise" (papers `sda.md` > 7. Open loop, not closed). The ACDC architecture checks revocation state "without exposing a point of validation (PoV)" (ACDC spec > TEL Registrars and Observers, L1693), a named rejection of OCSP and CRLs ([chapter 07, section 3.1](07-shibboleths-and-anti-patterns.md#crl-ocsp)).

#### Discovery is separated from trust {#discovery-vs-trust}

KERI finds an identifier's KEL through Out-Of-Band Introductions (OOBIs), URIs associated with an AID, and treats them as untrusted: "an OOBI itself is not trusted but MUST be verified... any information obtained from the service endpoint provided in the OOBI MUST be verified by some other mechanism" (KERI spec, L2629). Because what an OOBI delivers is end-verifiable, KERI "does not, therefore, need its own dedicated discovery network; OOBIs with URLs will do," and may "safely use DNS/CA, web search engines, social media, email, and messaging as discovery mechanisms. The worst case is the OOBI fails" (KERI spec, L2635; KERI spec > OKEA, L2954). Even the multi-namespace bootstrap pattern "does not change the trust model of an OOBI" (KERI spec > OOBI with MFA, L2838 @`fbdd4a615`); [chapter 06, section 2](06-governance-ecosystems-and-interop.md#oobi-mfa-arbitration) has the detail and [chapter 11](11-infrastructure-and-operations.md#infrastructure) the operations. Finding data is untrusted; verifying it is self-contained.

## 5. The three-layer stack: KERI, CESR, ACDC — and why each exists {#three-layer-stack}

KERI is the "Who" (identity), ACDC the "What" (data), and CESR "the language they speak" (papers `keri-primer.md` > 3. ACDCs; 4. CESR). Each layer exists because a property could not be had otherwise.

### 5.1 KERI — the identity / key-management layer {#keri-layer}

KERI answers who controls this identifier now and how anyone can know it, contributing the AID, the KEL, pre-rotation, witnesses, delegation and the duplicity model. Pre-rotation makes an AID survivable: "even if a hacker can abuse a current key, they can't rotate to new keys and take permanent control without accomplishing a second hack somewhere else" (papers `x509-prob.md` > Prerotation is missing).

### 5.2 CESR — the encoding / interchange layer {#cesr-layer}

CESR is "a dual text-binary encoding format that has the unique property of text-binary concatenation composability" (CESR spec > SAD Path Signatures, L1396): concatenated primitives convert as a group between text and binary "without loss" (CESR spec > Composability, L5), which naive Base64, padding each primitive separately, cannot do: it gives only "one-way composability" (CESR spec, L252). CESR "was developed for the KERI protocol" (CESR spec > SAID, L1186) but is "not limited to merely encoding Cryptographic Primitives" (CESR spec > Abstract Domain, L11). It exists as a layer for compact codes suited to constrained devices (KERI spec > Compact labels, L257), for crypto agility in which a new algorithm claims an unused code and "old ones will break if they receive the new ones" while new implementations accept old streams (CESR spec > Protocol genus/version codes, L618), and for deterministic parsing of interleaved JSON, CBOR and MGPK (CESR spec > Stream parsing rules, L427). Agility makes migration "an event in the KEL. Not a global infrastructure project" (keri-security-analysis `cr-ad-trust.md` > 6. The Transition Problem: A Comparison of Trajectories), and it has a ceiling: "There is a saying in crypto. Too much agility is a bad thing. There is a goldilocks amount of agility." (CESR spec GitHub > #165, comment of 2026-03-19, R-018, https://github.com/trustoverip/kswg-cesr-specification/pull/165#issuecomment-4086516133) [proposal]. [Chapter 04](04-cesr-and-the-wire.md#goldilocks-agility) owns CESR.

### 5.3 ACDC — the authentic-data / credential layer {#acdc-layer}

An ACDC is an ordered, SAID-bearing field map "issued by" and "issued to" AIDs (ACDC spec > Targeted Attribute Section, L318), so authentic data is always attributable to a KERI-rooted controller. ACDCs are "fragments of a globally distributed property graph" (ACDC spec > ACDCs as secure graph fragments, L1060-1068), and "No shared or trusted relationship between the Controllers and Verifiers is REQUIRED" (ACDC spec > AID Fields, L95). They are "not just a format — they're a *methodology* for creating verifiable evidence" (papers `x509-prob.md` > Much better alternatives exist). ACDC is the lossless archival form and W3C VC, SD-JWT and mDL are lossy distribution forms, so "once you've chosen a lossy format as your primary evidence, you cannot reconstruct what you've lost" (papers `acdc-vc-diff.md` > Lossless vs. Lossy: A Choice That Matters). The recommended posture keeps the lossless ACDC authoritative and generates lossy formats when an ecosystem requires them (papers `acdc-vc-diff.md` > The Right Format for the Job). [Chapter 05](05-acdc-and-verifiable-data.md#what-an-acdc-is) owns ACDC.

### 5.4 SAID — the connective tissue {#said}

A SAID is "a special type of content-addressable identifier based on an encoded cryptographic digest that is self-referential" (CESR spec > SAID, L1184), and "Any sufficiently strong cryptographic commitment to a content-addressable identifier is functionally equivalent to a cryptographic commitment to the content itself" (CESR spec > SAID, L1188). A signature on a SAID therefore commits to the whole serialization (KERI spec, L306), and "a SAID will verify if and only if its encompassing serialization has not been mutated, which makes the content immutable" (CESR spec > SAID, L1196): "An ACDC is not a mutable document; it is a crystallized fact" (papers `keri-primer.md` > 3.2 SAIDs). SAIDs let KELs anchor arbitrary data, let ACDCs reference each other across trust domains, and let CESR carry compact commitments to large structures. Canonical order is insertion order, not lexicographic: "The natural canonical ordering for such mappings is insertion order" (CESR spec > Order-Preserving Data Structures, L1250), against JCS-style canonicalization ([section 7](#mainstream-divergence)).

## 6. The X.509 / PKI problem KERI answers {#pki-problem}

### Identifier separated from its keys {#identifier-key-separation}

In X.509 an identifier "is a record in a database. The key is a separate artifact. The binding between them is an assertion", which "places the root of trust outside the identity itself" (keri-security-analysis `cr-ad-trust.md` > 1. The Identity Problem: Administrative Trust and Its Limits). The KERI specification states it as a verifiability failure: a certificate gives "evidence of authenticity of the assignment... but not evidence of the veracity of the mapping" (KERI spec > Overcoming existing security overlay flaws, L81). And rotation has no cryptographic link: "When rotating the valid signing keys, there is no cryptographically Verifiable way to link the new (rotated in) controlling/signing key(s) to the prior (rotated out)" (KERI spec, L83). CRLs and OCSP do not fix this — "This merely is asserted." — and re-issuing a certificate "is essentially the same as starting over by creating a brand-new independent mapping" (KERI spec, L83). Google Cloud's 2026 advice not to pin certificates, because pinning breaks at routine rotation, shows it: "there is no cryptographic thread joining the old key to the new one, so a relying party that pinned the old key pinned nothing durable" (papers `x509-prob.md` > Prerotation is missing) ([chapter 07, section 2.4](07-shibboleths-and-anti-patterns.md#pinning)).

Further PKI failures the corpus names:

- The wrong locus of risk: "*Certificate authorities aren't the ones that choose or manage the keys in certs.* Holders of certs do that" (papers `x509-prob.md` > Governance is opaque, centralized, and inconsistent, L65 @`181569b64`), so the risk lives in opaque holder key management, a "missile silo" guarded by "one opaquely managed secret" (same section, L49 and L51).
- Evidence that expires by design: "Evidence of identity expires by design, not by necessity" (keri-security-analysis `cr-ad-trust.md` > 1. The Identity Problem: Administrative Trust and Its Limits), which "created a strong incentive to take the insecure and easy path: if there's a risk of a compromised key, just wait until the cert expires" (papers `x509-prob.md` > Revocation is disincented): "The organizations most in need of urgent revocation are the ones most likely to wait" (same).
- Fail-open revocation: when an OCSP responder is down, browsers "accept the unverifiable certificate as valid because blocking traffic is considered too disruptive", so "The system is most vulnerable precisely when it is most needed" (papers `keri-primer.md` > 1.1 Fragility of centralized roots).
- Certificate Transparency redistributes trust. It "is a detection mechanism, not a prevention mechanism" (papers `keri-primer.md` > 1.1 Fragility of centralized roots), with a merge-delay window and an ultimate arbiter that is "a handful of browser vendors" (papers `wtbo.md` > 1. The Identity Problem: Administrative Trust and Its Limits) with "immediate, global effect... no formal appeals process" (papers `wtbo.md` > Notes, [b]). "The result is not a decentralized trust hierarchy. It is a more elaborate one" (keri-security-analysis `cr-ad-trust.md` > 2. Certificate Transparency: Progress and Its Limits) ([chapter 07, section 3.3](07-shibboleths-and-anti-patterns.md#certificate-transparency)).
- Jurisdiction-bound trust: "Assertions rooted in the SHAKEN ecosystem mandated by US regulators are not accepted in Europe or Asia" (papers `keri-primer.md` > 1.1 Fragility of centralized roots).

#### The retrograde attack, the deepest contribution {#retrograde-attack}

Bare signatures are "difficult to sequence relative to a compromise or revocation event" (papers `was.md`, intro). An attacker who obtains a key once can forge evidence "that looks like it originated in the past, *forever*", and this remains possible "even if she rotates or revokes K before Malfoy steals it" (papers `was.md`, unheaded opening, L18–33 @`181569b64`). X.509, JWTs, OAuth2, OIDC, SD-JWTs and current-state-only DIDs share the limit: "you can ask whether one is valid *now*... but you can't ask whether it was valid a week ago" (papers `was.md` > Mitigation 3 (pyrrhic victory)). NIST SP 800-102 agrees that a signed message "provides no assurance that the private key was used to sign the message at that time" (papers `keri-primer.md` > 3.3 Anchored vs. paired signatures). KERI's answer is anchored signatures: "Keep tamper-evident records that can prove how a given signing event relates in time to changes in the associated key state" (papers `was.md` > Solution), anchored in the KEL, after which a rotation means "Malfoy's attack window closes forever" (same), and which need "no total ordering, and no cumbersome consensus algorithm" (same). [Chapter 03, section 11](03-key-management-and-identifier-lifecycle.md#as-of-anchor) owns the mechanism.

The boundary the corpus states: this is a migration-cost argument, not present-day parity. X.509 has legal standing KERI lacks, "a gap of legal recognition, not of technical capability" (papers `wtbo.md` > Notes, [c]), and "KERI's oft-cited operational complexity is itself load-bearing" (papers `wtbo.md` > 6. The Transition Problem: A Comparison of Trajectories). That KERI's constraints are of immaturity while PKI's are architectural is a falsifiable, contestable position.

## 7. The decentralization-purity thesis and where the mainstream diverged {#mainstream-divergence}

The corpus describes generations of identity evidence: X.509 as 1.0, DIDs and W3C VCs as 2.0, ACDC and KERI as 3.0, "in production since December 2022, when the first global-scale systems went to production" (papers `x509-prob.md`). The drift critique is that SD-JWT, W3C VCs and most DID methods imported PKI and OAuth assumptions and "re-created centralization one level up while losing time, trust-chain, and key-history fidelity" (raw/06 section 9). Its author "worked on both of these standards, and I remain proud of their virtues" (papers `x509-prob.md` > Much better alternatives exist), so it is an insider's critique. The divergences:

- DIDs stalled on issuer rotation. "Evidence is also *issued by* an identifier... If the issuer updates their identifier, old evidence is invalidated", and "This risk is poorly understood in SSI circles" (papers `x509-prob.md` > Much better alternatives exist), like a licensing bureau "whose recover-from-breach plan required all current license holders to come back to the office" (papers `was.md`) ([chapter 07, section 6.2](07-shibboleths-and-anti-patterns.md#dids-solved-it)).
- W3C VCs are lossy in six ways: the trust chain collapses to one hop, VCs are "generally verifiable only in the present", edges cannot carry properties, the three-party model fits pure attestation badly, issuer key history is opaque, and `@context` link rot lets meaning change "without breaking the cryptographic signature" (papers `acdc-vc-diff.md`).
- SD-JWT signs the wrong thing: its `kid` "points at the key, not the identifier, so key rotation invalidates historical signatures" (papers `sdjwt-acdc.md` > 2.3 Signing mechanism), and its standalone envelopes "will need registries of trusted issuers" (papers `sdjwt-acdc.md` > 2.4 Chaining), where ACDCs "prove the bona fides of issuers by chaining to other ACDCs." Its flexibility in identifier type "makes it much more difficult to predict or enforce security properties" (papers `sdjwt-acdc.md` > 2.2 Identifiers). [Chapter 06, section 8](06-governance-ecosystems-and-interop.md#scorecard) has the comparison.
- Canonicalization: JCS "is brittle," and JSON-LD term redefinition means "the meaning of a signed credential can be altered without invalidating the signature" (papers `keri-primer.md` > 4.1 The engineering bottleneck: text vs. binary) ([chapter 07, section 6.4](07-shibboleths-and-anti-patterns.md#json-canonicalization)).
- Blockchain reflexes: anchoring needs only per-identifier ordering, with "no permissioning problem, no big central *anything* in the sky, no scale or performance bottlenecks, and no regulatory problems with data locality, privacy, or erasure" (papers `was.md` > Solution); forgetting an identifier on a blockchain "requires a hard fork of the entire chain" (papers `keri-primer.md` > 3.5 Privacy and selective disclosure), whereas KERI's separate logs let a user delete a KEL "without disrupting the global ecosystem" (same) ([chapter 07, section 4.1](07-shibboleths-and-anti-patterns.md#global-consensus)).

### Most DIDs resolve only to current key state {#did-current-state}

"most DIDs, including crowd favorites like did:web, can only be resolved to the current key state" (papers `was.md` > Mitigation 3 (pyrrhic victory)), so historical actions cannot be verified unless the method supports `versionId` or `versionTime`, which most do not. That exposes them to the retrograde attack of [section 6](#retrograde-attack). The conversion runs one way: "AIDs can be transformed to DIDs, but the opposite transformation is typically impossible" (papers `sdjwt-acdc.md` > 2.2 Identifiers).

Beneath these is one architectural fork. In closed-loop systems "the party that *issues* authority is the same party that *checks* it"; in open-loop systems "a third party who is *not* the issuer can verify authority *without* consulting the issuer" (papers `sda.md` > 7. Open loop, not closed). KERI and ACDC are open-loop, the authority-side form of end-verifiability, and the drift toward registries, status endpoints and current-state resolution is a slide back to closed-loop trust.

Cryptography's reach is bounded by authenticity against veracity: "Judgments about authenticity can — *if managed very carefully* — be reduced to an objective mathematical computation, whereas judgments about veracity inherently require subjective assessments of reputation" (papers `authenticity-vs-veracity.md`). KERI mechanizes authenticity and does not claim veracity ([chapter 06, section 1](06-governance-ecosystems-and-interop.md#authenticity-veracity)), a discipline the applied schema corpus restates [practice]: an ACDC's validation "can prove that an issuer remains committed to a citation — but only the interaction context... plus governance... establishes what proposition is true" (bakobo/schema `citation/index.md` > Citations > Semantic precision, L39 @`cfcbdcc7a`). That is why a human keeps the final judgment: "Automate the introduction. Automate the proof where you can. But never automate the verification of the proof" (papers `oia.md` > Connections, and the line between introducing and proving, L86 @`181569b64`).

## 8. Well-established vs. contested: the load-bearing assumptions {#load-bearing-assumptions}

### 8.1 Well-established: definitional or cryptographically grounded {#well-established}

- The self-certifying derivation, KEL structure, pre-rotation and SAID anchoring are specified and implemented, resting on standard hash and signature assumptions ("A1 — Cryptographic Soundness," keri-security-analysis `background.md` > 4.6 Explicit Assumptions Introduced in This Section) and roughly 128-bit strength targets (KERI spec > Annex A, L1698-1704).
- Survivability is a stated design goal: "KERI, as specified, adopts a survivability-oriented security model" (keri-security-analysis `standard-analysis.md` > 0. Objective-Function Alignment), so faulting it for not preventing malicious-controller duplicity is a category error ([chapter 02, section 1](02-security-model-and-threat-posture.md#survivability)).
- The post-quantum argument for pre-rotation is sound in shape: an attacker "must first invert the digest... Pre-quantum cryptographic strength is, therefore, not weakened post-quantum" (CESR spec > Post-Quantum Security, L1510-1512; KERI spec, L1711-1713). The "effective 128 bits" number attached to it is contested ([chapter 02, section 5](02-security-model-and-threat-posture.md#pq-hedge)). Regulation is moving the other way on classical signatures: the EU catalog's draft demotes them to a time-limited class, so "staying classical becomes a rationale owed for every new system rather than a wall met on a date" (papers `wtbo.md` > 6. The Transition Problem: A Comparison of Trajectories, L147 @`181569b64`).

### 8.2 Contested, conditional, or load-bearing assumptions {#contested}

[Chapter 02](02-security-model-and-threat-posture.md#assumptions) owns the security assumptions and their evidence. In summary: KERI does not guarantee global convergence, and permanent divergence between validators is allowed ([chapter 02, section 10](02-security-model-and-threat-posture.md#local-validity)); KAWA's consistency holds only under an honest controller, who may otherwise run its own witnesses ([chapter 02, section 7](02-security-model-and-threat-posture.md#kawa)); the observer layer that detection depends on is optional, unenforced and immature, and "A KERI deployment without robust watchers and governance is not a secure system" (keri-security-analysis `cr-ad-trust.md` > 5. Evaluating KERI: Real Tradeoffs, Honestly Stated) ([chapter 02, section 9](02-security-model-and-threat-posture.md#observer-layer)); centralized "super watcher" archives pull back toward the dependency KERI avoids; double compromise of current and rotation keys is unrecoverable by design; and the strongest formal analysis is an unreviewed 2025 Master's thesis, whose findings the corpus resolves by its own argument rather than by independent consensus.

#### Adversarial review by AI personas is not independent validation {#ai-review-not-validation}

A source in this corpus says so about itself: passes over `m-glance.md`'s model "were conducted by AI models prompted to argue from a named lens, not by human domain experts", and "They are a structured way to attack one's own assumptions, not independent validation" (papers `m-glance.md` > 1. Introduction, L71–74 @`181569b64`). The paper is not a KERI source, but the point applies on the same terms to adversarial-review output produced for this repository, including `keri-doctrine.md`'s panels. It is evidence about what the method's output is worth, not that any claim here is wrong.

#### A signed presentation is a durable correlator {#durable-correlator}

When a credential is shown to infrastructure rather than only to a counterparty, the evidence is identical at every party that sees it, which makes "a conventional signed presentation a durable correlator across matchers and across queries", and "unlinkable presentation that still permits counting is not a solved problem" (papers `active-discovery.md` > Proving who is asking, L113 @`181569b64`). The recommended mitigation is architectural: "The narrower and safer arrangement is for the seeker's evidence to travel end-to-end and be evaluated only by the sought party" (same line). This bounds graduated disclosure once a third party sees a presentation at all, and it is the author conceding the gap in his own voice.

### 8.3 Properties stated relative to assumptions {#relative-to-assumptions}

Security properties are always stated relative to their assumptions: "Agreement thresholds do not imply global safety... Failure to state these boundaries results in overstated guarantees" (keri-security-analysis `background.md` > 4.7 Analytical Implications; 5.10 Analytical Boundary Conditions). KERI's worldview is internally consistent on its own survivability terms. The open questions are whether watchers, governance, legal recognition and tooling mature fast enough to deliver in practice the detection and recovery the design permits but does not compel.

## 9. Where the doctrines came from {#lineage}

Each doctrine has a dated origin in Sam Smith's papers, which are [proposal]: whitepapers, several co-authored, none specification text. Where Sam's later narration differs, the papers are the record (raw/20 section 7.1).

The security frame came first: "Survivability can be broken up into three aspects, these are, susceptibility, vulnerability, and recoverability." (Smith Papers `Identity-System-Essentials.pdf` > Security), the triad of [chapter 02, section 1](02-security-model-and-threat-posture.md#survivability). That 2016 paper rested security on ledgers, "Using diffuse trust distributed consensus enables the decentralized and highly secure management of many parts of the identity system" (Smith Papers `Identity-System-Essentials.pdf` > Hybrid Ledgers), a position KERI later reversed, so it is history, not doctrine.

Pre-rotation took two steps. In 2017 Self-Identifying Data listed "The four R's of key managment are:" (Smith Papers `SelfIdentifyingData.md` > Identifier Key Management, L171; the misspelling is the source's), with no pre-commitment. In 2018 Decentralized Autonomic Data folded revocation into rotation, "We call these the essential three R's of key management." (Smith Papers `DecentralizedAutonomicData.md` > Key Management, L108), and introduced the mechanism: "The approach presented here is to pre-rotate the DID key and declare the pre-rotation at the inception of the DID." (Smith Papers `DecentralizedAutonomicData.md` > Basic Pre-rotation, L228) ([chapter 03, section 5](03-key-management-and-identifier-lifecycle.md#pre-rotation)). The same paper's Replicants, redundant hosts of an event history, are the ancestors of witnesses, and it rejects a consensus ledger because it "is not the minimally sufficient means of providing this capability" (Smith Papers `DecentralizedAutonomicData.md` > Replayability, L427). The co-authored Cooperation Beats Aggregation still endorsed "blockchain-anchored decentralized identity infrastructure" in 2019 (Smith Papers `CooperationBeatsAggregation.md` > Conclusion, L258), a lag in a jointly written paper rather than Sam's position that year.

ACDC chaining comes from A DID for Everything (2018), where "Data flows may be provenanced via a self-referential blockchain" (Smith Papers `A_DID_for_everything.md` > Motivation, L99), each item carrying the identifier and signature of the one before ([chapter 05, section 5](05-acdc-and-verifiable-data.md#edges)). Identifier theory and reputation, the purpose all of it serves, run from Open Reputation (2015) to Universal Identifier Theory (2020) and belong to [chapter 14, Identifiers, Trust Bases and Reputation](14-identifiers-trust-bases-and-reputation.md#identifiers).
